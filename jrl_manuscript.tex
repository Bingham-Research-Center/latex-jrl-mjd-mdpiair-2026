\abstract{Cold-pool structure controls mixing of surface, wintertime ozone in the mountainous Uinta Basin, Utah, USA. However, point surface values alone do not uniquely establish that structure.
We evaluated deterministic sensitivity in Weather Research and Forecasting simulations of the 2 February 2013 Uinta Basin episode to factors such as 3D grid-spacing, terrain resolution, initial conditions (GFS, NAM), and boundary-layer parameterisation.  
After model spin-up, GFS-driven members were warmer at the surface, 3--4\,K less stable in the 500m--50m layer above ground, and approx. 1.0 m\,s$^{-1}$ faster in fixed-layer transport wind than NAM members.
GFS-driven simulations also created a weaker cold pool (measured by Whiteman heat deficit); yet, as every simulation strengthened the cold pool, this suggests initial-state offset over rapid GFS cold-pool loss.
Other effects, such as slope shading, two-way-nest feedback, and terrain fineness had effects an order of magnitude smaller than the initial conditions. Important for the mechanism of cold-pool building, where smoother terrain encourages cold, dense air to drain faster to the basin floor, but volume is lower as smaller canyons are undersampled. 
While results do not isolate, e.g., initial and lateral boundary condfitions, or verify inversion depth, its primary output is reviewing best practices for preparing meteorology dataset before air-chemistry models (CMAQ, WRF-CHEM) begin integration.}

\section{Introduction}

Surface wintertime ozone in the Uinta Basin can build after heavy snowfall and a period of quiescent weather encourages formation of a persistent cold-air pool (Lareau CITE). Wintertime ozone is rare, documented in a handful of places in the US (CITE, CITE), China (CITE, CITE), etc. As emissions from local oil and gas industry is trapped, even weak insolation builds ozone concentrations to unhealthy levels via photochemistry worsened by actincic flux from high-albedo snow. Mechanisms are further explored in \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm,Neemann2015-ws,Davies2025-wy}: a common theme is poorly simulated meteorology, given emissions investories are satisfactory (Lyman or Mansfield cite?) and air-chemistry simulations are otherwise trustworthy (Loknath cite?).
Hence, for a coupled model, key information lies not only in the 2-m temperature error at Basin sites, but also whether the simulated atmosphere contains a faithful, shallow cold pool with weak transport during the high-ozone periods. Without this, air-chemistry models are strongly bounded in their fidelity. 

The scientific literature around cold pools and wintertime air-quality in complex terrain has only moderate consensus when ranking relevant uncertainties and their importance for atmospheric simulation. 
Ahmadov et al. simulated a higher ozone concentration after applying an aircraft-derived emissions scaling --- holding meteorological prescription constant --- and later conditioned the resulting inventory interpretation on adequate meteorology \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm}. (Look at critique - is this a problem?)
Matichuk et al. evaluated cumulative experiments that modified volatile-organic-compound (VOC) emissions, leaf area, and (what kind of?) deposition together. Alas, improved WRF-derived vertical temperature profiles did not consistently improve ozone in the vertical \citep{Matichuk2017-bq}.
Existing error amongst air chemistry factors (confinement, emissions, photolysis, deposition, reaction mechanism) demands meteorological evaluation and optimisation that is necessary for chemical attribution (i.e., reducing the dominance of meteorology as an error source in air-quality simulations). 
(A common theme is adjusting with obs, but we show ICs are a bigger problem, Thunderstorms Don't get butterflies... one exception is the smoothness of snow field and inability to 'bogus in' snow? Problem without more snow sensors --- Davies cite; others on low snow coverage in remote US.)
(The rapid advance of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) forecasting systems demands rigorous analysis of traditional numerical simulations, and whether the compute power of WRF is "worth it".)

Other meteorological experiments warn against a lack of rigour: Neemann et al. found large case-specific responses after bundling choices in snow, albdeo, and microphysics parameterisation suite. However, their ice-retention modification was successful in tightening uncertainty bounds. There are outstanding questions regarding grid spacing: their innermost 1.33-km grid, subject to most evaluation, was finer than the 4 km meteorology supplied to the chemistry model \citep{Neemann2015-ws}. We briefly address 3D grid resolution herein.
Tran et al. found meteorological response to four-dimensional data assimilation depended on nudged variables, input data, and diagnosed boundary-layer depth (itself an inconsistent function of the parameterisation namelist chosen). However, the corrections by nudging (see critique points). Further, there is a cycle of citations without solid proof. This study is a first step in a fresh start (cite BRC reports).

Experiments herein have been chosen as interesting or representative of common threads in preceding studies.
Simulations were chosen as controlled contrasts, organised by the dial(s) that revealed the contrast or scientific surprise in question.
Following preliminary experiments and review of internal and published reports, we ask the following scientific questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item What is the nature and magnitude of impact from two-way feedback between nested domains? (Not used in Tran or Neemann: one-way) has little impact (more so in convective, turbulent?).
  \item When contrasting simulations with coarse- and fine-terrain, how does cold-pool evolution and stability change?

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Case, Observations, and Evaluation Domains}

Our case of interest is a period of high ozone within a larger January--February 2013 window in the Uinta Basin. We focus on six hours after sunrise of 2 February 2013, where a snow-covered Basin cultivated a persistent cold-air pool (Tran ref, Fig XX) and high ozone levels (Fig. XX) that exceeded the 8-hour National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) limit of 70\,ppb. This is a case covered by multiple previous air-chemistry studies \citep{Neemann2015-ws,Ahmadov2015-zm,Tran2018-xx}.
All common-sample contrasts were initialised at 1200 UTC, running six hours, corresponding to 0700--1100 Mountain Standard Time (MST; UTC-7).
The first output time ($\tau$=0\,min), representing interpolated coarse initial conditions, is excluded when evaluating post-spin-up model behaviour. 
Surface observations were obtained from Synoptic Data (cite; website?) for 2-m temperature (11 sites), 10-m wind speed (10 sites), and mean sea-level pressure (9 sites). 
Subhourly reports were mean-averaged within each UTC clock hour.
The stations span basin-floor, sidewall, and bench locations but are neither spatially uniform nor area weighted; as the focus herein is not verification against observations, we leave further evaluation to future studies.
The domains are shown in Fig XX. The 1-km d02 was selected for some observation comparisons, given 4 stations lie outside the innermost domain. 
Figure~\ref{fig:v3-locator} combines the reporting-station geometry with the full nested-domain layout.
The use of 1-km instead of innermost 333-m grids does not ensure both domains generate a similar response, and again, deeper analysis is outside scope of this study. 
Four weather stations spanning the foothills' altitude difference provide surface context from 31 January 0000 UTC to 4 February 0000 UTC (Fig. XX).
Observed and modelled surface potential temperature for a two-site structural index was calculated as $\theta=T(1000/p)^{0.2854}$, with $T$ in kelvin and $p$ in hPa, and the index was defined as Mountain Home on the northwest bench minus Pariette Draw on the basin floor (locations labelled in Fig. XX).
A caveat is the horizontal separation between the two sites, requiring scepticism of the index as a near-surface contrast, rather than free-air inversion strength, depth of cold air, etc.

Episode-scale ozone context came from the current EPA Air Quality System (AQS) AirData daily archive for 1 January--28 February 2013.
We retained the EPA-reported ``Ozone 8-hour 2015'' daily-summary row at Ouray, Redwash, Roosevelt, Vernal, and Little Mountain. 
All five sites contained one daily row on each of the 59 local calendar dates; within-day observation coverage ranged from 47\% to 100\% and is marked explicitly in the figure.
The satellite panel in Fig. XX, taken from the NASA Global Imagery Browse Services Terra MODIS satellite (CITE), shows corrected-reflectance bands (\textit{7-2-1 layer}) for 2 February 2013 --- the widespread snow coverage is apparent in cyan shading.

\subsection{WRF Configuration and Archived Simulation Family}
\label{sec:v3-family}

The controlled six-hour simulations used WRF version 4.8.0, initialised 1200 UTC, using either Global Forecast System (GFS) or North American Model (NAM) analyses from 12:00 to 18:00 UTC on 2 February 2013. The use of analyses to provide the bookend initial and boundary conditions are therefore "best guesses", not output from a six-hour forecast, and is why we underscore these runs represent simulations rather than forecast system guidence (i.e., any real-world evaluation would be an optimistic skill measurement).
We used three nested domains with horizontal grid spacing of 3\,km, 1\,km, and 333\,m. For increased sampling in the boundary layer, we prescribed 75 eta levels that were stacked in the PBL with \approx20\,m separation. All simulations set a 50 hPa model top.
The reference physics suite used Thompson microphysics (REF), RRTMG longwave and shortwave radiation (REF), the revised MM5 surface layer (REF), the Noah land-surface model (REF), and the YSU planetary-boundary-layer scheme (REF). The suite was chosen as a common (i.e., default) namelist configuration as a na\"ive starting point that displayed numerical stability in preliminary testing.
Grid and observation four-dimensional data assimilation were disabled (i.e., no nudging as in Tran et al (REF)).
Departures from that reference configuration are listed by simulation in Table~\ref{tab:v3-family}.

\subsection{Surface and Common-AGL Diagnostics}

Modelled 2-m drybulb temperature, 10-m wind, and MSLP were sampled at the nearest d02 cell to each station. None of the cells touched on steep terrain or obviously unrepresentative model locations.
Across the eleven fixed columns, model terrain offset from reported station elevation spanned -32\,m to 14\,m.
While this justifies the nearest-neighbour approach, the pairing of WRF output to observation value does not remove (wind, solar) exposure, slope angle, land-cover use, or network-coverage deficiency.
Mass-level height above ground was calculated from (perturbation plus base) geopotential values against local WRF terrain height (i.e., interpolated onto WRF grid from terrain dataset).
Grid-relative winds were destaggered and rotated to earth-relative components.
Potential temperature $\theta$ and 10-m wind components were interpolated linearly at 25 m increments (50--500\,m AGL) at each station-site column. 
Fixed-layer stability was defined as (CITE?):

\begin{equation}
S_{50:500}=\theta(500\,\mathrm{m})-\theta(50\,\mathrm{m})
\end{equation}

The transport-wind proxy is defined as (CITE?):

\begin{equation}
$U_{50:500}=\{\bar{u}^{2}+\bar{v}^{2}\}^{1/2}
\end{equation}

where the bars denote height means from 50 to 500 m AGL.
These wind-derived values are crude proxiesn and do not rigorously sample flow through the basin boundary, pollutant residence time, cold-pool volume, or a chemistry-model ventilation coefficient (deferred to a future study).
Native WRF planetary-boundary-layer (PBL) height was retained only as a scheme-dependent companion diagnostic; the reader may find caveats discussed by Tran (REF) highlight pitfalls in PBL height diagnoses for the MYJ/Eta configuration.

\subsection{Pairing, Aggregation, and Short-Sample Sensitivity}

Run differences were paired by station and valid hour before spatial aggregation.
Each fixed station field was reduced to a single unweighted spatial median per hour; the five-hour value is thus a mean of those five medians.
The stations therefore define one sampled spatial field and are not treated as independent replicates.
A descriptive circular moving-block calculation drew 20,000 resamples of the five hourly medians with two-hour blocks and reports the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles as a sensitivity interval.
Due to the single case and high sensitivity to multiple factors, the authors focus more on practical significance rather than the statistical kind, and do not deliver extensive confidence testing herein.

\subsection{Footprint-Integrated Heat Deficit}
\label{sec:v3-heatdeficit-methods}

We estimate basin-volume heat deficit following Whiteman et al. (REF), whose value $\Delta H$ represents the sensible heat required to remove a basin temperature inversion.
A close implementation of the column diagnostic is found in Adler et al. (REF).
Let $p_{s}$ and $p_{c}$ denote the pressures at the surface and fixed crest reference, respectively, with $p_{c}<p_{s}$.
The present area-normalised atmospheric heat-deficit proxy was defined as $H=(c_{p}/g)\int_{p_{c}}^{p_{s}}\max\{\theta_{\mathrm{crest}}-\theta(p),0\}\,dp$, where $\theta_{\mathrm{crest}}$ is the potential temperature interpolated to the crest height in each modelled column.
$H$ is reported as a cell-area-weighted mean over an evaluation footprint in MJ\,m$^{-2}$.
The footprint is the convex hull of the eleven evaluation stations (registered basin waypoints), intersected with model terrain below the crest reference height.
The definition of Whiteman's $H$ herein prescribed a 2200\,m crest; with the convex hull, the sampling area covers \approx5000 cells and \approx5300\,km$^{2}$.
To check sensitivity to the crest value, we ran experiments using six alternate definitions, varying crest height from 2100 to 2300\,m. 
A further check dilated or erode the boundary by one cell, and did not substantially affect results. Similarly, changes to the convex hull area and waypoint location was immaterial to the general result, giving confidence to initial conclusions below. 
The footprint is an evaluation region rather than a hydrologic basin boundary, and the proxy is neither a direct reproduction of the published diagnostics nor an observed cold-pool measurement. Other definitions of the Basin, in political and geographic terms, are discussed in (Lin ref) etc.

\subsection{Exploratory X8 Deficit-Transport Diagnostic}
\label{sec:v3-x8-methods}

As an independent feasibility study for resolving flow that is \textit{O}(500\,m), we ran a single (X8) run with a fourth (d04) nest that output in 10\,min intervals during the same time period (1200--1800). Due to concern over numerical instability, we pair the $\Delta = 111\,\text{m}$ with a more conservative 50 vertical levels, rather than 75.
The horizontal heat-deficit transport was defined as (REF):

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}=(c_{p}/g)\int_{p_{c}}^{p_{s}}\max\{\theta_{\mathrm{crest}}-\theta(p),0\}\mathbf{u}_{h}(p)\,dp
\end{equation}

using the same 2200\,m crest reference-value; then $\mathbf{F}$ has units of W m$^{-1}$, and $-\nabla_{h}\!\cdot\mathbf{F}$ is reported only as the horizontal flux-convergence contribution to the tendency of $H$.
Storage was differenced over each 10 min interval; horizontal convergence was mean-averaged at the interval endpoints; both were area-weighted after a symmetric 15-cell edge crop from d04 for stability of solution and ignoring spurious edge effects.
The apparent differences are difficult to resolve: any effect from diabatic physics, the time-varying local crest reference, clipped-layer movement, vertical and boundary terms, and numerical error can contribute to confound effect attribution.

We also introduce deficit-weighted velocity (REF):

\begin{equation}
$\mathbf{U}_{D}=\mathbf{F}/H$ was masked where $H<0.1$ MJ m$^{-2}
\end{equation}

An active-layer depth used a 0.25 K minimum crest-relative deficit.
The displayed reduced-gravity Froude number is an exploratory bulk proxy rather than rigorous diagnosis.
Three short west-to-east transects cross model-terrain incisions; one longer west-to-east line provides a northern view.
A positive-signed transport denotes southward movement.
As elsewhere, 1200--1400 UTC is omitted other than spin-up context, whereas 1400--1800 UTC is retained for interpretation.

\subsection{Driving-Product Response in the Common Lower Layer}

The two-by-two diagnostic visualisation (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-retention}) separates the driving meteorology (NAM or GFS) with/without two-way feedback, giving a foundation "control" for effects of differing initial conditions. 
Figure XX shows GFS--NAM differences in 50--500 m potential-temperature stability, with NAM colder by 3.63 K (two-way feedback) and 3.65\,K (no feedback), highlighting the negligible effect of feedback between domains for this particular study. (See Table~\ref{tab:v3-effects}).
The corresponding five-hour means (OF WHAT?) were 7.50 and 7.38 K for both GFS runs, compared with 10.82 K for both NAM members.
The five hourly spatial-median differences ranged from -5.58 to -1.13 K and from -5.36 to -0.90 K, respectively.
All ten product-by-hour stability contrasts therefore retained the same sign: absolute values are less truthworthy in such a sensitivity experiment, hence consistency of sign is a stronger signal of robustness.
The GFS--NAM comparison found GFS increased fixed-layer transport wind by 0.79\,m s$^{-1}$ (feedback on) and 0.99\,m s$^{-1}$ (feedback off).
The mean values (OF WHAT?) were \approx2.0\,m s$^{-1}$ (both GFS members) versus \approx1.1\,m s$^{-1}$ for NAM members.
The hourly differences ranged 0.37--1.18\,m s$^{-1}$ (0.61--1.50\,m s$^{-1}$ for no feedback) with consist sign between hours. 
Native PBL-height differences changed sign within both feedback modes and did not reproduce the stability and wind separation.
As noted elsewhere, PBL parameterisation idiosyncrasies for this case are discussed in Tran (REF, page).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The Main Result Is a Difference in the Supplied Lower-Layer State}

The present study does not seek a ranking of performance in this case, but rather clear results regarding the cold pool's representation in WRF as a function of numerous dials used in air-chemistry simulations. 

\begin{itemize}
  \item The GFS-driven runs supplied a warmer, less stable, and faster-moving 50--500 m layer than analagous NAM members (with and without feedback enabled).
  \item The Whiteman heat-deficit calculations support the same ordering and conclusion of GFS presenting an initial dataset less conductive to a strong cold pool.
  \item This large (relative to the climatology; not shown) variation should signal to coupled air-quality modellers how suboptimal meteorology and model configuration can set up a chemistry simulation for failure (i.e., the inversion is mixed out: the cold pool is blown away)


The GFS state is more consistent with dilution than the analogous NAM run, but the transport-wind proxy and heat-deficit values are not full substitutes for basin export and observed mixing volume.
This distinction is necessary due to the inextricable snowballing of error in emissions, snow and cloud mechanisms, chemical deposition, chemical-reaction mechanisms, and meteorological "air-mass boundaries" can compensate for one another in coupled simulations \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm,Neemann2015-ws,Matichuk2017-bq}.
Ultimately, a search conducted under a mentality of "one true model" is risky due to low predictability of WRF simulations in complex terrain and sub-kilometre grid-spacing (inherent property of flow; Lorenz), and researchers are encouraged to run Monte Carlo (ensemble) simulation systems that vary dials shown most sensitive to the phenomenon of interest.

The model-only d03 Horsepool profiles at 12:00 and 13:00 UTC (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-spinup-profiles}) expose those supplied states but neither separate early adjustment from boundary evolution nor validate inversion depth. (DO WE NOT SEE A SPIN UP? FIGURE HERE?)

\subsection{The Difference Is a State Offset, Not Demonstrated Cold-Pool Loss}
\label{sec:v3-disc-spatial}

The heat-deficit result narrows the interpretation.
GFS had less deficit than NAM under every feedback and footprint definition, but deficit increased in every simulation during the retained window (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-heatdeficit-traj}).
The predeclared faster-loss test therefore failed, and the product separation must be described as a state offset already present by 14:00 UTC.
A longer integration through at least one complete diurnal cycle is required to test whether the products differ in cold-pool erosion or persistence.

The spatial analysis gives a similarly bounded result.
The product response was strongest below 500 m AGL and had the expected footprint-mean sign at every hour, but the two-thirds sign-coherence criterion failed at 14:00--15:00 UTC.
The response is shallow and mean-coherent on d02, not spatially uniform throughout the window.
Preserving these failed criteria is more informative than rescuing them with a new threshold after inspection.

\subsection{Observation and Design Limits Set the Next Experiment}
\label{sec:v3-disc-domains}

These outcomes motivate better measurements and future experiment design, but they do not yet support radiation or terrain-source superiority claims. 
The strongest result is the two-by-two anchor: GFS versus NAM with/without feedback. However, secondary results now inform further exploration, including during coupling with air-chemistry processing. Problems raised herein include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Artefacts in native PBL heights.} The reported PBL height is scheme dependent, and can show spurious values or artefacts from hard-gating rules (see Tran et al.; MORE ABOUT MYJ versus YSU PBLH diagnosis).
  \item \textbf{Observed floor-to-bench temperature contrast is valuable, but does not validate,} cold-pool (inversion) depth, layer stability, or modelled heat deficit. A three-dimensional picture is paramount throughout.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}

The largest repeated response in the controlled archive is the GFS-versus-NAM driving-product contrast on the common d02 evaluation sample.
Relative to NAM, GFS is warmer at the evaluated surface sites, about 3.6 K less stable from 50 to 500 m AGL, 0.8--1.0 m s$^{-1}$ faster in fixed-layer transport wind, and lower in footprint-integrated heat deficit under both feedback modes.
The heat-deficit separation is a state offset, not demonstrated faster loss, and the surface metrics do not support a universal product ranking.
Nest-feedback effects are much smaller, the PBL/surface-layer response is smaller and metric dependent, and the slope-radiation and fine-terrain screens do not pass their declared gates.
The next experiment requires a longer matched matrix, independent vertical observations, a common-support d02--d03 transfer test, and a design that separates initial from lateral-boundary forcing.
The present result does not identify the responsible product component, validate inversion depth, establish event-scale persistence, calculate basin export, or predict ozone.
Within those bounds, the driving-product treatment produced the largest repeated separation in the lower-basin meteorological state supplied to downstream chemistry during the five-hour window.
