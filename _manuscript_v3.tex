\abstract{Cold-pool structure controls the mixing volume and transport available to winter-ozone chemistry, but surface skill alone does not establish that structure.
We evaluated Weather Research and Forecasting simulations of the 2 February 2013 Uinta Basin episode, anchored by four runs that crossed GFS and NAM driving products with one-way and two-way nest feedback.
Across five post-spin-up hours, the GFS members were warmer at the surface, about 3.6 K less stable from 50 to 500 m above ground, and 0.8--1.0 m s$^{-1}$ faster in fixed-layer transport wind than the matched NAM members.
They also had less footprint-integrated heat deficit, but every simulation gained heat deficit during the window; the product separation was a state offset, not faster GFS cold-pool loss.
Nest-feedback effects were much smaller, and the boundary-layer/surface-layer treatment produced a smaller, metric-dependent response.
The design does not isolate initial from lateral-boundary forcing, validate inversion depth or event-scale persistence, or predict ozone.}

Winter ozone in the Uinta Basin develops when photochemistry proceeds within snow-covered, stably stratified air that restricts vertical dilution and horizontal exchange \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm,Neemann2015-ws,Davies2025-wy}.
Snow increases actinic flux and modifies deposition, while the stable, weakly ventilated air confines ozone and its precursors near the basin floor.
For a coupled model, the meteorological question is therefore not only whether 2 m temperature has a small mean error.
It is whether the atmosphere delivered to the chemistry grid contains a credible shallow mixing environment and weak transport during the accumulation period.

Published concentration agreement does not provide a clean ranking of the relevant uncertainties.
Ahmadov et al. obtained a much stronger ozone simulation after applying an aircraft-derived, Horsepool-centered emissions scaling while holding the meteorological simulation fixed, and they conditioned the resulting inventory interpretation on adequate meteorology \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm}.
Matichuk et al. evaluated a cumulative ladder that changed volatile-organic-compound emissions, leaf area, and deposition together, while improved WRF temperature profiles did not consistently improve ozone profiles \citep{Matichuk2017-bq}.
These outcomes are consistent with compensating error among confinement, emissions, photolysis, deposition, and chemical mechanism.
They make meteorological evaluation necessary for chemical attribution without making meteorology the uniquely dominant error source.

The meteorological literature also warns against reducing an experiment to a configuration label.
Neemann et al. showed strong case-specific responses from bundled snow, albedo, and cloud-microphysics choices, but their ice-retention modification was a bounding treatment and the 1.33 km grid evaluated most closely was finer than the 4 km meteorology supplied to the chemistry model \citep{Neemann2015-ws}.
Tran et al. showed that the meteorological response to four-dimensional data assimilation depended on the nudged variables, input data, and diagnosed boundary-layer depth; their disclosed artificial formaldehyde limits quantitative chemical inference \citep{Tran2018-ax}.
Thus, labels such as ``NAM driven,'' ``nudged,'' or ``high resolution'' do not by themselves identify a controlled cause, and aggregate scores can obscure a failure to retain the layer of interest.

The archive is not a chronological experiment sequence.
It is a graph of simulations connected by controlled contrasts, and we organize it by the lever that each contrast can identify.
The first question is whether the short-window lower-layer state responds reproducibly to the GFS-versus-NAM driving-data product under both nest-feedback modes.
The second is how the two process treatments represented in the fine-terrain branch---slope-aware radiation and a coupled boundary-layer/surface-layer scheme change---alter that state.
The third is whether the within-product feedback response remains small at both default and fine terrain.
The fourth is whether changing only the static-terrain source produces a coherent basin-floor or terrain-organized response.
For the driving-product contrasts we additionally ask whether the response appears in a footprint-integrated heat-deficit measure and whether the products differ in its within-window change.

Contrasts that share the frozen station and common-height pipeline are displayed together before any individual result is developed.
Terrain-source contrasts instead require area-weighted geometry diagnostics, because a station-only summary can miss a rim- or slope-confined response.
The stable X0--X10 and CMP01--CMP12 codes are retained for provenance, but run numbers do not set manuscript order or evidential weight.
The heat-deficit comparison carries two criteria that were fixed before any output was inspected, and all executed gates retain their pass or fail outcome.
The design evaluates the meteorological input state that a later chemistry calculation would receive; it does not contain a chemistry calculation.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Case, Observations, and Evaluation Domains}

The target is the snow-covered Uinta Basin cold-air pool on 2 February 2013 within the episode examined by prior meteorological and chemical studies \citep{Neemann2015-ws,Ahmadov2015-zm}.
All common-sample contrasts use 14:00--18:00 UTC, corresponding to 07:00--11:00 mountain standard time.
The first two forecast hours were excluded as spin-up, leaving five hourly samples; the sensitivity of the results to that two-hour choice has not been evaluated.
Surface observations were extracted from the Synoptic archive for eleven stations reporting 2 m temperature, ten reporting 10 m wind speed, and nine reporting surface pressure in every retained hour.
Subhourly reports were averaged within each UTC clock hour.
The stations span basin-floor, sidewall, and bench locations but are neither spatially uniform nor area weighted.
Every controlled simulation discussed below archived both the 1 km d02 and the 333 m d03 during this window, so output availability did not determine the evaluation domain.
The 1 km d02 was selected for the common observation-linked estimand because it contains all eleven stations, whereas seven stations clamp to the boundary of the roughly 50 by 48 km d03 and only four provide usable interior columns there (ledger row C03).
Figure~\ref{fig:v3-locator} combines the reporting-station geometry with the full nested-domain layout.
This coverage decision does not establish that d02 resolves every process response adequately or that a response measured on d02 has the same magnitude or ordering on d03.
The present results therefore describe the full-network d02 sample; a d03 analysis would require a separately declared interior footprint or the same four-station subset on both domains and is not a resolution evaluation.
Four elevation-spanning stations provide surface context from 31 January 00:00 UTC through 4 February 00:00 UTC.
Observed and modeled surface potential temperature for a two-site structural index was calculated as $\theta=T(1000/p)^{0.2854}$, with $T$ in kelvin and $p$ in hPa, and the index was defined as Mountain Home on the northwest bench minus Pariette Draw on the basin floor.
Because the two sites are horizontally separated and locally exposed, the index is a spatial surface contrast rather than a colocated free-air inversion strength or depth.
No sounding, profiler, or remote-sensing profile is used to validate the modeled 50--500 m layer.

\subsection{WRF Configuration and Archived Simulation Family}
\label{sec:v3-family}

The controlled six-hour simulations used WRF version 4.8.0 from 12:00 to 18:00 UTC on 2 February 2013.
They used three nested domains with horizontal grid spacing of 3, 1, and 0.333 km, 75 eta levels, and a 50 hPa model top.
The reference physics suite used Thompson microphysics, RRTMG longwave and shortwave radiation, the revised MM5 surface layer, the Noah land-surface model, and the YSU planetary-boundary-layer scheme.
Grid and observation four-dimensional data assimilation were disabled.
All deliberate departures from that reference configuration are listed by simulation in Table~\ref{tab:v3-family}.
The GFS one-way member reused the six verified metgrid files from the GFS two-way member before rerunning \texttt{real.exe} and \texttt{wrf.exe}.

The table is ordered by scientific role rather than identifier so that archive chronology does not determine manuscript prominence.

\subsection{Design Exclusions: Resolution and Legacy Context}
\label{sec:v3-resolution}

The archive contains runs whose innermost grids span nominal 1 km, 333 m, and 111 m spacing (X7, X0, and X8), and a visually tempting ``resolution ladder'' could be drawn from them.
That ladder would be false, and this is a confirmed design finding (C16): the three configurations differ simultaneously in initialization and forecast age, domain count and placement, feedback mode, vertical levels, terrain treatment, radiation options, and output cadence (Table~\ref{tab:v3-confounds}).
No grid-spacing response can be attributed within this set, and nominal fine spacing is not evidence of a better cold-pool simulation.
A controlled resolution experiment would require a new matched family; X7 and X8 therefore do not enter the Results comparison hierarchy.
Legacy nudged and SIP-era simulations are likewise heterogeneous in episode, model version, and assimilation state and remain context only.
Appendix~B applies the within-X8 diagnostic in Section~\ref{sec:v3-x8-methods} to illustrate the subhourly transport structure available in that archive without converting X8 into comparative evidence.

\subsection{Surface and Common-AGL Diagnostics}

Modeled 2 m temperature, earth-relative 10 m wind, and surface pressure were sampled at the nearest d02 cell to each station.
Across the eleven fixed columns, model terrain minus station elevation had a median of -2.0 m and ranged from -32.4 to +14.3 m.
That agreement limits one obvious mismatch but does not remove exposure, slope, land-cover, or network-coverage error.
Mass-level height above ground was calculated from perturbation and base-state geopotential and the local WRF terrain height.
Grid-relative winds were destaggered and rotated to earth-relative components.
Potential temperature and both wind components were interpolated linearly at 25 m increments from 50 to 500 m above ground level (AGL) at each station column.
Fixed-layer stability was defined as $S_{50:500}=\theta(500\,\mathrm{m})-\theta(50\,\mathrm{m})$.
The transport-wind proxy was $U_{50:500}=\{\bar{u}^{2}+\bar{v}^{2}\}^{1/2}$, where the bars denote height means from 50 to 500 m AGL.
This wind metric is not flow through the basin boundary, pollutant residence time, cold-pool volume, or a chemistry-model ventilation coefficient.
Native WRF planetary-boundary-layer (PBL) height was retained only as a scheme-dependent companion diagnostic.

\subsection{Footprint-Integrated Heat Deficit}
\label{sec:v3-heatdeficit-methods}

The original basin-volume heat deficit of Whiteman et al. represents the sensible heat required to remove a basin inversion, and Adler et al. provide the closest published column implementation to the present diagnostic.
\rewritegate{Insert the Paperpile-generated keys for Whiteman et al. (1999) and Adler et al. (2023) after John's small curated export; do not invent or hand-edit keys.}
The present area-normalized atmospheric heat-deficit proxy was defined as $H=(c_{p}/g)\int\max\{\theta_{\mathrm{crest}}-\theta,0\}\,dp$, integrated from the surface to a fixed crest reference height, where $\theta_{\mathrm{crest}}$ is the potential temperature interpolated to that height in each modeled column.
$H$ is reported as a cell-area-weighted mean over an evaluation footprint in MJ m$^{-2}$.
The footprint is the convex hull of the eleven evaluation stations and eleven registered basin waypoints, intersected with model terrain below the crest reference.
The primary definition uses a 2200 m crest and the combined hull, covering 4965 d02 cells and 5277 km$^{2}$.
Six alternate definitions vary the crest to 2100 and 2300 m, dilate or erode the boundary by one cell, or restrict the hull to the stations or waypoints alone.
Because two-way feedback modifies the d02 terrain field by up to 23.1 m relative to the static one-way field, differences were formed within feedback mode, X6 minus X5 and X10 minus X3, on a common mask fixed from the one-way NAM terrain; the members of each pair share identical grids.
Hourly values at 13:00 UTC provide trajectory context only, and all decisions use 14:00--18:00 UTC.
Two criteria were fixed before inspection: a weaker-state criterion requiring both GFS-minus-NAM pairs to be negative at every retained hour under all seven definitions, and a faster-loss criterion requiring both GFS 14:00-to-18:00 UTC changes to be negative and more negative than the matched NAM changes under all seven definitions.
The fixed 2200 m crest, nonnegative clipping, convex-hull footprint, and modeled profiles depart from the published definitions.
The footprint is an evaluation region rather than a hydrologic basin boundary, and the proxy is neither a direct reproduction of the published diagnostics nor an observed cold-pool measurement.

\subsection{Exploratory X8 Deficit-Transport Diagnostic}
\label{sec:v3-x8-methods}

The single X8 context run archived d04 output every 10 min from 12:00 to 18:00 UTC on a nominal 111.111 m grid with 50 vertical levels.
It is multiply confounded relative to the controlled family and is used only to demonstrate a within-run process diagnostic, not a resolution response or configuration ranking.
The horizontal heat-deficit transport was defined as $\mathbf{F}=(c_{p}/g)\int\max\{\theta_{\mathrm{crest}}-\theta,0\}\mathbf{u}_{h}\,dp$, using the same local 2200 m MSL crest reference and nonnegative kernel as $H$.
$\mathbf{F}$ has units of W m$^{-1}$, and $-\nabla_{h}\!\cdot\mathbf{F}$ is reported only as the horizontal flux-convergence contribution to the tendency of $H$.
Storage was differenced over each 10 min interval, horizontal convergence was averaged at the interval endpoints, and both were area weighted after a symmetric 15-cell edge crop from d04.
Their difference is called the unresolved tendency because diabatic physics, the time-varying local crest reference, clipped-layer movement, vertical and boundary terms, and numerical error can all contribute.
The deficit-weighted velocity $\mathbf{U}_{D}=\mathbf{F}/H$ was masked where $H<0.1$ MJ m$^{-2}$.
An active-layer depth used a 0.25 K minimum crest-relative deficit, and the displayed reduced-gravity Froude number is an exploratory bulk proxy rather than a hydraulic-control diagnosis.
Three short west-to-east transects cross model-terrain incisions and one longer west-to-east line provides a northern reference; positive signed transport is southward, and none of these lines is a basin boundary.
As in the controlled analyses, 12:00--14:00 UTC is shown as spin-up context and only 14:00--18:00 UTC is retained for interpretation.

\subsection{Driving-Product Response in the Common Lower Layer}

The common-AGL diagnostics separated the driving products under both feedback settings (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-retention}).
GFS minus NAM reduced 50--500 m potential-temperature stability by 3.63 K under two-way feedback and 3.65 K under one-way feedback (Table~\ref{tab:v3-effects}).
The five hourly spatial-median differences ranged from -5.58 to -1.13 K and from -5.36 to -0.90 K, respectively.
All ten product-by-hour stability contrasts therefore retained the same sign.
GFS minus NAM increased fixed-layer transport wind by 0.79 m s$^{-1}$ under two-way feedback and 0.99 m s$^{-1}$ under one-way feedback.
The hourly differences ranged from +0.37 to +1.18 m s$^{-1}$ and from +0.61 to +1.50 m s$^{-1}$, with the same sign in every hour.
Native PBL-height differences changed sign within both feedback modes and did not reproduce the stability and wind separation.

\subsection{Process Treatments on the Fine-Terrain Branch}
\label{sec:v3-process}

The scheme pair (CMP08, X2$-$X1) changes only the boundary-layer and surface-layer schemes, MYJ/Eta against YSU/MM5, while holding the fine terrain and slope-aware radiation configuration fixed.
MYJ/Eta increased 2 m-temperature bias by 0.37~$^{\circ}$C, reduced 10 m-wind absolute error by 0.145 m s$^{-1}$, and increased fixed-layer transport wind by 0.098 m s$^{-1}$, with the same sign in all five hours (Table~\ref{tab:v3-process}; C32).
These changes are smaller than the driving-product response on the shared lower-layer metrics, and the opposing temperature- and wind-error changes do not support a scheme ranking.
PBL height is scheme defined and is not compared as a common physical layer.

The slope-radiation treatment produced essentially no change at the basin-floor station columns (C31).
An aspect-organized skin-temperature response appeared on the sidewalls, but the archived flat-plane shortwave field and missing surface heat fluxes could not test the mechanism directly.
The predeclared screen therefore failed for a measurement-limited reason.
The diagnostic is retained in Appendix~A (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-radiationpanel}); it does not support a main-text claim that slope radiation improved or degraded the cold pool.

\subsection{Nest-Feedback Robustness across Two Terrain Regimes}
\label{sec:v3-feedback}

\label{sec:v3-cmp06}
Within-product feedback effects were small beside the repeated GFS-minus-NAM separation on the default-terrain matrix (Table~\ref{tab:v3-effects}).
The fine-terrain pair reproduced that bound: stability changed by +0.04 K and transport wind by -0.01 m s$^{-1}$, while their sensitivity intervals spanned zero (Table~\ref{tab:v3-cmp06}; C28).
Small, consistently signed wind-error and pressure changes do not support a configuration recommendation.
Nest feedback therefore serves as a replicated robustness check for this case and window, not as a principal result or a claim of general equivalence.

\subsection{Terrain-Source Brackets (CMP09--CMP11)}
\label{sec:v3-terrain}

The archive brackets the static terrain source on an otherwise identical NAM one-way configuration: default minus fine (CMP09, X3$-$X0), default minus coarse (CMP10, X3$-$X4), and fine minus coarse (CMP11, X0$-$X4).
The default-minus-fine terrain change was small on d02, and its 0.07--0.27 K response failed the predeclared terrain-correlation criterion early in the window (Table~\ref{tab:v3-terrain-summary}; C29).
The coarse-terrain brackets produced larger responses, but no criterion was declared for them before inspection, so they remain diagnostic rather than evidence for a terrain ranking.

At 16:00 UTC, the grid-cell median X0-minus-X4 heat-deficit difference was +0.014 MJ m$^{-2}$ on d02, with 53.4\% of cells positive, and -0.184 MJ m$^{-2}$ on d03, with 64.2\% of cells negative.
The shared-support below-crest section medians were +0.278 K north--south and +0.369 K west--east.
These support- and diagnostic-dependent summaries execute no new gate, rank no terrain source, and establish neither a drainage pathway nor a change in basin cold-air volume.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The Main Result Is a Difference in the Supplied Lower-Layer State}

The clearest result is not that one simulation wins a surface-score contest.
It is that the GFS-driven members supplied a warmer, less stable, and faster-moving 50--500 m layer than the matched NAM members under both feedback settings.
The footprint heat-deficit calculation supports the same ordering beyond the fixed station columns.
The failed spatial-coherence screen in Table~\ref{tab:v3-spatial-summary} records the qualification hidden by the footprint aggregate.
Replication across feedback makes the product separation robust to that configuration choice within this matrix, while the opposite pressure-error ordering prevents a universal product ranking.

This distinction matters to an air-quality model because meteorology sets the layer in which ozone and its precursors mix and move.
The GFS state is more consistent with dilution than the matched NAM state, but the transport-wind proxy is not basin export and the heat-deficit footprint is not an observed mixing volume.
No chemical species were simulated, so the result identifies a meteorological precondition for attribution rather than an ozone response.
That boundary is necessary because emissions, snow and cloud treatment, deposition, chemical mechanism, and meteorological confinement can compensate for one another in coupled simulations \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm,Neemann2015-ws,Matichuk2017-bq}.

The driving-product treatment also bundles the 12:00 UTC initial state with later boundary updates.
The model-only d03 Horsepool profiles at 12:00 and 13:00 UTC (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-spinup-profiles}) expose those supplied states but neither separate early adjustment from boundary evolution nor validate inversion depth.
The present design cannot assign the lower-layer response to one of those components.
``Driving-product response'' is therefore warranted; ``initial-condition effect'' is not.

\subsection{The Difference Is a State Offset, Not Demonstrated Cold-Pool Loss}
\label{sec:v3-disc-spatial}

The heat-deficit result narrows the interpretation.
GFS had less deficit than NAM under every feedback and footprint definition, but deficit increased in every simulation during the retained window (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-heatdeficit-traj}).
The predeclared faster-loss test therefore failed, and the product separation must be described as a state offset already present by 14:00 UTC.
A longer integration through at least one complete diurnal cycle is required to test whether the products differ in cold-pool erosion or persistence.

The spatial analysis gives a similarly bounded result.
The product response was strongest below 500 m AGL and had the expected footprint-mean sign at every hour, but the two-thirds sign-coherence criterion failed at 14:00--15:00 UTC.
The response is shallow and mean-coherent on d02, not spatially uniform throughout the window.
Preserving these failed criteria is more informative than rescuing them with a new threshold after inspection.

\subsection{Secondary Experiments Narrow Rather than Expand the Claim}
\label{sec:v3-disc-process}

The process and geometry comparisons are useful chiefly because they delimit the main result.
The common-pipeline overview in Figure~\ref{fig:v3-effects} makes that response hierarchy explicit on the shared metrics.
The boundary-layer/surface-layer treatment produced a coherent but smaller response, with temperature error worsening while wind error improved.
It is a process-treatment contrast, not evidence that one named scheme is generally superior.
Nest-feedback effects remained small at both default and fine terrain, so feedback protects the product result as a robustness check without becoming a second thesis.

The slope-radiation and fine-terrain screens did not earn main-text figures.
Slope radiation was nearly invisible at the basin-floor stations, while its sidewall skin-temperature signal could not be tested against a slope-corrected radiative or surface-flux diagnostic.
The default-minus-fine terrain response was small and failed its predeclared correlation criterion; the much wider fine-minus-coarse bracket is controlled and visually stronger, but it had no predeclared scientific gate.
The CMP11 distributions change with domain support and diagnostic, so the composite does not establish redistribution, drainage, an upstream reservoir, or a terrain-source ranking.
A future terrain test requires a predeclared common-support metric, time-resolved heat-deficit and transport diagnostics, and independent vertical structure.
These outcomes motivate better measurements and future experiment design, but they do not yet support radiation or terrain-source superiority claims.
The multiply confounded X7/X0/X8 set is excluded for the same reason: nominal grid spacing cannot be isolated from its other configuration changes.
Within X8 alone, the 10-min diagnostic resolves a strong modeled morning northward pulse across the northern reference line followed by relaxation (Appendix~B).
Because X8 has no matched control and the line is not a basin boundary, this is process context rather than evidence of basin export, resolution sensitivity, or model skill.

\subsection{Observation and Design Limits Set the Next Experiment}
\label{sec:v3-disc-domains}

The common-AGL result leads because it applies one geometric layer, station sample, time window, and aggregation to all four anchor simulations.
Native PBL height cannot replace it because PBL height is scheme dependent and its product contrasts changed sign.
The observed floor-to-bench temperature contrast is valuable surface corroboration, but horizontally separated stations do not validate inversion depth, layer stability, or modeled heat deficit.
Observed ozone and satellite snow cover will strengthen the episode context, not supply that missing vertical validation or establish a meteorology--ozone causal response.

The d02 choice is likewise a sampling decision rather than a resolution result.
It retains all eleven stations, whereas d03 retains only four usable interior columns, but it may smooth slope, tributary, and terrain-edge responses.
A predeclared transfer test must compare d02 and d03 over the same geographic support and the same four-station sample before the present response hierarchy is assumed to hold at 333 m.

The five-hour window, fixed two-hour spin-up exclusion, dependent hourly summaries, and incomplete observation provenance limit every conclusion.
The next discriminating experiment should combine a longer matched product/feedback matrix with an independent in-basin vertical reference and a design that separates initial from lateral-boundary forcing.
Only then should selected meteorological states be crossed with emissions and chemical mechanisms to test an ozone response.
All failed gates in the present archive remain failed until new evidence is generated under a newly declared criterion.

\section{Conclusions}

The largest repeated response in the controlled archive is the GFS-versus-NAM driving-product contrast on the common d02 evaluation sample.
Relative to NAM, GFS is warmer at the evaluated surface sites, about 3.6 K less stable from 50 to 500 m AGL, 0.8--1.0 m s$^{-1}$ faster in fixed-layer transport wind, and lower in footprint-integrated heat deficit under both feedback modes.
The heat-deficit separation is a state offset, not demonstrated faster loss, and the surface metrics do not support a universal product ranking.
Nest-feedback effects are much smaller, the PBL/surface-layer response is smaller and metric dependent, and the slope-radiation and fine-terrain screens do not pass their declared gates.
The next experiment requires a longer matched matrix, independent vertical observations, a common-support d02--d03 transfer test, and a design that separates initial from lateral-boundary forcing.
The present result does not identify the responsible product component, validate inversion depth, establish event-scale persistence, calculate basin export, or predict ozone.
Within those bounds, the driving-product treatment produced the largest repeated separation in the lower-basin meteorological state supplied to downstream chemistry during the five-hour window.
