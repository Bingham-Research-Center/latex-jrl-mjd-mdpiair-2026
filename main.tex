\abstract{Cold-pool structure controls the confinement and lower-atmospheric flow relevant to wintertime ozone in Utah's Uinta Basin, but surface observations alone cannot resolve that structure.
We evaluated Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) simulations of 2 February 2013 for sensitivity to Global Forecast System (GFS) or North American Mesoscale (NAM) forcing, nest feedback, boundary-layer and surface-layer schemes, slope-aware radiation, and static terrain.
Across five matched hours, GFS-driven runs were warmer at the evaluated surface sites, had an approximately 3.6\,K smaller 50--500\,m potential-temperature endpoint difference, and a 0.8--1.0\,m\,s$^{-1}$ larger layer-mean wind-vector magnitude than feedback-matched NAM runs.
GFS also retained less footprint-mean heat deficit under all seven definitions.
Every run gained heat deficit from 1400 to 1800 UTC, despite within-window declines, so the product separation represents a state difference without demonstrated faster cold-pool loss.
Feedback and physics responses were smaller and metric dependent; terrain diagnostics revealed spatial responses that the station sample could miss.
The results guide meteorological diagnostics for subsequent air-chemistry modelling, while leaving the initial-versus-lateral forcing contribution, inversion depth and basin export unresolved.}

\keyword{winter ozone; WRF simulation; meteorological uncertainty; cold-air pool; complex terrain; model resolution}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:v3-introduction}

Wintertime ozone in the Uinta Basin develops within snow-covered, stable lower-atmospheric layers that confine precursors near the surface \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm,Neemann2015-ws,Davies2025-wy}.
The basin floor, benches and surrounding terrain support strong temperature contrasts and thermally driven slope flows \citep{Lyman2015-aq,Whiteman2000}.
Comparable winter ozone episodes occur in Wyoming's oil-and-gas-producing basins \citep{Oltmans2014-md}.
Longer Uinta records show changing ozone and precursor conditions \citep{Mansfield2020-kl}; here we examine the meteorology of one 2013 episode over five retained hours.

Surface observations alone cannot validate inversion depth or the three-dimensional mixing volume supplied to a chemistry model.
Evaluation therefore requires complementary diagnostics of potential temperature, lower-layer winds and heat storage, with a clear distinction between observed agreement and modelled structure.

Earlier Uinta modelling demonstrates strong case dependence.
Snow cover can strengthen cold pools by increasing albedo, while cloud phase alters longwave forcing and nocturnal cooling \citep{Neemann2015-ws}.
Snow, microphysics and grid choices can thus alter both the cold pool and its representation on evaluation and chemistry grids.
Four-dimensional data assimilation (FDDA) depends on the nudged variables, input analysis and scheme-dependent boundary-layer diagnosis \citep{Tran2018FDDA}.
A subsequent agency study examined reinitialisation, coordinates, snow initialisation and nudging, but assimilated evaluation data and differing samples limit configuration comparisons \citep{Tran2021-al}.
These studies motivate the present sensitivity tests.

Meteorological, emissions, photolysis, deposition and chemical-mechanism errors can compensate in coupled simulations.
Changing emissions with fixed meteorology can improve ozone agreement without uniquely identifying inventory error \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm}.
Cumulative changes to these processes complicate attribution, and improved temperature profiles need not improve ozone profiles \citep{Matichuk2017-bq}.
Credible meteorology reduces this confounding but does not guarantee accurate ozone.

We test whether the GFS--NAM lower-atmospheric response recurs under one-way and two-way nest feedback, and compare it with physics, radiation and terrain responses.
The diagnostics combine surface skill, the 50--500\,m potential-temperature endpoint difference, layer-mean horizontal wind-vector magnitude and footprint-mean heat deficit.
Together they describe the simulated cold-pool state; they do not measure inversion depth, ventilation or basin export.

We ask:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Does the GFS--NAM lower-layer response recur under both feedback modes?
  \item How do feedback, boundary-layer and surface-layer schemes, and slope-aware radiation compare with the forcing-product response?
  \item How does changing the static-terrain source affect the atmosphere on the same WRF grid?
  \item What do the combined diagnostics reveal that a single station metric can miss?
\end{enumerate}

The study contains no chemistry calculation and does not separate initial from lateral-boundary forcing.
Planned follow-on work led by L.D. will connect meteorological representation with air-chemistry modelling for this and related cases.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:v3-methods}

\subsection{Case, Observations, and Evaluation Domains}
\label{sec:v3-case}

The case falls within the January--February 2013 period of snow cover, cold pools and elevated Uinta ozone \citep{Neemann2015-ws,Ahmadov2015-zm,Tran2018FDDA}.
WRF integrations cover 1200--1800 Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), or 0500--1100 Mountain Standard Time (MST; UTC$-7$), on 2 February.
Excluding the initialised output and first-hour forecast leaves the longest common window, 1400--1800 UTC (0700--1100 MST), with five hourly samples.
Synoptic Data PBC Mesonet observations provide 2\,m temperature at 11 sites, 10\,m wind speed at 10 sites and surface pressure at 9 sites \citep{SynopticTimeseries}.
The temperature sample includes every registered station reporting in the window; Sand Wash had no reports.
Subhourly reports were averaged within each UTC clock hour.
The stations span the basin floor, sidewalls and benches but are unevenly distributed and provide no independent free-air vertical validation.
We used the 1\,km d02 domain because seven stations lie outside the usable d03 interior (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-locator}); a common-support d02--d03 comparison remains future work.
Four stations provide context from 31 January 0000 UTC to 4 February 0000 UTC.
For the observed and modelled Mountain Home-minus-Pariette Draw index, we calculated surface potential temperature as $\theta=T(1000/p)^{0.2854}$, with $T$ in kelvin and $p$ in hPa, using the dry-air Poisson relation \citep{WallaceHobbs2006}.
This horizontally separated bench-minus-floor index measures a surface contrast, not free-air inversion strength or depth.

Ozone context uses the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Air Quality System (AQS) AirData daily archive for January--February 2013 \citep{EPAAirData}.
We retained the ``Ozone 8-hour 2015'' daily-summary row at Ouray, Redwash, Roosevelt, Vernal and Little Mountain: one row per site on each of 59 local dates, with within-day coverage of 47--100\% marked in the figure.
The satellite panel uses the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Global Imagery Browse Services (GIBS) Terra Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) corrected-reflectance 7-2-1 layer for 2 February \citep{NASAGIBS}.
Interpretation uses the spectral absorption and scattering principles described by \citet{Liou2002}, with snow/cloud colours specified by NASA's layer documentation \citep{NASAMODIS721}.

\subsection{WRF Configuration and Archived Simulation Family}
\label{sec:v3-family}

We used WRF version 4.8.0 \citep{Skamarock2019}, initialised at 1200 UTC with lateral boundary conditions through 1800 UTC on 2 February 2013.
The inputs were the 1200 and 1800 UTC zero-hour GFS grid 004 (0.5$^{\circ}$; \texttt{gfsanl\_4}) or NAM grid 218 (12\,km; \texttt{namanl\_218}) analyses \citep{GFSAnalysis,NAMAnalysis}.
These retrospective integrations include temporally interpolated lateral-boundary forcing, so the retained response combines initial-state and boundary influences.
Three nested domains, d01--d03, have horizontal spacings of 3\,km, 1\,km and 333.333\,m, with 75 eta levels, approximately 20\,m spacing near the surface and a 50\,hPa model top.
The reference physics comprises Thompson microphysics \citep{Thompson2008-ti}, Rapid Radiative Transfer Model for General Circulation Models (RRTMG) longwave and shortwave radiation \citep{Iacono2008}, the revised fifth-generation Pennsylvania State University--National Center for Atmospheric Research Mesoscale Model (MM5) similarity surface layer \citep{Jimenez2012Surface}, the Noah land-surface model \citep{Tewari2004} and the Yonsei University (YSU) planetary boundary layer (PBL) scheme \citep{Hong2006}.
Grid and observation FDDA were disabled.
Table~\ref{tab:v3-family} lists the simulations analysed here.

Static terrain entered through the WRF Preprocessing System and was fixed within each non-terrain comparison.
The fine, default and coarse sources have spacings of 3 arc seconds, 30-arc-second class and 5 arc minutes, respectively; the coarse source is GMTED2010 \citep{DanielsonGesch2011}.
These correspond to approximately 90\,m, 900\,m and 9\,km north--south spacing at $40^{\circ}$N, distinct from WRF grid spacing.
Snow and other land-surface initial fields entered through product-specific preprocessing, so their contributions remain part of the GFS--NAM treatment.

\subsection{Experiment Comparisons}
\label{sec:v3-contrasts}

The primary comparison crosses GFS and NAM forcing with one-way and two-way feedback on default terrain (X3, X5, X6 and X10).
GFS and NAM inputs supply 27 and 40 metgrid levels, respectively.
Additional pairs test boundary-layer and surface-layer schemes, slope radiation, feedback on fine terrain, and terrain source; each is introduced with its result.
Within each pair, the evaluation domain, station or footprint sample, valid hours and diagnostic calculation are matched.

\subsection{Surface and Common-AGL Diagnostics}
\label{sec:v3-common-agl}

Surface variables and vertical fields were sampled from the same nearest d02 column at each station, keeping the model terrain and profile consistent.
Station-to-cell distances are 133--594\,m, with default-terrain elevation offsets of $-32.4$ to $+14.3$\,m (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v3-stations}).
These pairings retain differences in exposure, slope and land cover; alternative interpolation methods were not tested.
Mass-level height above ground level (AGL) was calculated from perturbation plus base geopotential relative to local terrain.
Winds were destaggered and rotated to earth-relative components.
Potential temperature was interpolated at the layer endpoints; wind components were linearly interpolated every 25\,m from 50 to 500\,m AGL for height integration.
We repeated the four-run, eleven-column, five-hour comparison for 50--250 and 100--500\,m layers and 10 and 50\,m wind integration increments.
The bounds define a common shallow layer rather than an observed inversion boundary.
The increments test sampling of the same interpolated winds; they leave the potential-temperature endpoints unchanged and do not test native vertical resolution or interpolation method.

The finite-layer potential-temperature endpoint difference was defined as

\begin{equation}
S_{50:500}=\theta(500\,\mathrm{m})-\theta(50\,\mathrm{m})
\end{equation}

An increase of potential temperature with height indicates dry static stability \citep{WallaceHobbs2006,Stull1988}, but the endpoint difference cannot resolve inversion height, sharpness or internal layering.

The magnitude of the layer-mean horizontal wind vector was defined as

\begin{equation}
U_{50:500}=\left(\bar{u}^{2}+\bar{v}^{2}\right)^{1/2},
\end{equation}

where the bars denote height means from 50 to 500 m AGL, not temporal or turbulent averages.
We also calculated the layer-mean scalar speed $\overline{(u^{2}+v^{2})^{1/2}}$ and the vector-to-scalar ratio to identify directional cancellation.
These state diagnostics do not measure basin-boundary flux or pollutant residence time.
Native WRF PBL height serves as a scheme-dependent companion diagnostic \citep{Tran2018FDDA}.

\subsection{Pairing, Aggregation, and Short-Sample Sensitivity}
\label{sec:v3-aggregation}

Run differences were paired by station and valid hour, then reduced to one unweighted spatial median per hour.
Reported five-hour effects are means of those medians; stations are not independent replicates.
To explore temporal dependence \citep{Wilks2019}, a circular moving-block calculation drew 20,000 resamples of the five medians using two-hour blocks.
Its 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles are descriptive sensitivity bounds, not confidence intervals for this single deterministic case.

\subsection{Footprint-Integrated Heat Deficit}
\label{sec:v3-heatdeficit-methods}

We adapted the basin heat-deficit formulation of \citet{Whiteman1999} and the column implementation of \citet{Adler2023}.
Using hydrostatic balance, $dp=-\rho g\,dz$, to express column mass in pressure coordinates \citep{WallaceHobbs2006}, we defined
\begin{equation}
H=\frac{c_{p}}{g}\int_{p_{c}}^{p_{s}}\max\{\theta_{\mathrm{crest}}-\theta(p),0\}\,dp,
\end{equation}
where $c_{p}$ is dry-air specific heat at constant pressure, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and $p_s>p_c$ are surface and crest-reference pressures.
The reference potential temperature is interpolated to the fixed crest height in each modelled column; clipping retains only positive deficits.
This potential-temperature proxy describes stratification relative to that reference, rather than an exact energy requirement for removing the cold pool.
We report its cell-area-weighted footprint mean in MJ\,m$^{-2}$.
The primary footprint is the convex hull of eleven stations and eleven basin waypoints, restricted to terrain below 2200\,m: 4,965 d02 cells covering 5,277\,km$^{2}$.
Seven predeclared definitions use this hull at 2100, 2200 and 2300\,m; the 2200\,m hull dilated or eroded by one cell; and station-only and waypoint-only hulls at 2200\,m.
Feedback-matched GFS--NAM pairs share a terrain mask.
The weaker-state criterion requires a negative GFS-minus-NAM difference at every retained hour under all seven definitions.
The faster-loss criterion requires GFS heat deficit to decline from 1400 to 1800 UTC and to decline more than NAM under all seven definitions.
Both criteria preceded output inspection; 1300 UTC is trajectory context only.
The modelled footprint is an evaluation region, not a measured or hydrologically defined basin volume.

\subsection{Observed Surface-Temperature Context}
\label{sec:v3-observed-context}

The basin-floor station repeatedly separated from the higher sites between 31 January and 3 February, with diurnally varying temperature differences (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-observed}).
Seep Ridge (USU04; 1933\,m) has a distinct trace despite a station-to-cell distance of only 133\,m and a +2.9\,m terrain offset.
Exposure, slope flow and representativeness are possible explanations, but the metadata do not distinguish them.
A fresh retrieval returned all 17 quarter-hour samples for temperature, humidity, wind speed and direction without provider QC flags, but no sensor-position or exposure history.
It neither independently validates the measurements nor reproduces historical-cache processing.
The recurring floor-to-bench separation is consistent with a cold-pool surface pattern; the horizontally separated sites cannot determine its vertical structure.

\subsection{Driving-Product Response in the Common Lower Layer}
\label{sec:v3-driving-response}

GFS produced a smaller 50--500\,m potential-temperature endpoint difference than NAM under both feedback modes (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-retention}).
Paired GFS-minus-NAM effects averaged $-3.63$\,K with two-way feedback and $-3.65$\,K with one-way feedback, retaining their sign at every hour (Table~\ref{tab:v3-effects}).
Layer-mean wind-vector magnitude increased by 0.79 and 0.99\,m\,s$^{-1}$, respectively, also with consistent hourly signs.
Scalar-speed differences were similar, so directional cancellation does not create the speed ordering (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v3-wind-cancellation}).
The vector-to-scalar ratio difference changed sign in two of five hours for each pair.

Temperature and wind retain their hourly orderings across all nine diagnostic configurations.
Their magnitudes depend on layer bounds (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v3-layer-sensitivity}), whereas changing the integration increment at fixed bounds alters the mean wind effects by less than 0.02\,m\,s$^{-1}$.
Native PBL-height differences change sign under both feedback modes, consistent with the diagnostic's scheme dependence \citep{Tran2018FDDA}.

\subsection{Surface and Spatial Corroboration}
\label{sec:v3-surface}

GFS was warmer at the surface than NAM, consistent with the common-layer diagnostics (Table~\ref{tab:v3-surface-summary}).
The 2\,m temperature-bias contrasts were approximately +1.8\,$^{\circ}$C under both feedback modes.
Pressure absolute error decreased, while hourly wind-error differences changed sign.
Figure~\ref{fig:v3-bulk-theta} compares the observed two-site surface index with the two-way simulations.

The five-hour mean Mountain Home-minus-Pariette Draw contrast was 16.94\,K observed, 17.03\,K in NAM and 9.95\,K in GFS.
NAM's agreement supports this surface index without validating inversion depth or overall skill.
Across the footprint, GFS-minus-NAM near-surface responses were positive on average and concentrated below 500\,m AGL under both feedback modes (Table~\ref{tab:v3-surface-summary}).
The spatial-coherence test nevertheless failed: only 64--67\% of cells had the expected sign at 1400--1500 UTC, below the required two-thirds share at every hour.
Later coherence does not change that outcome.

\subsection{Footprint Heat Deficit}
\label{sec:v3-heatdeficit}

GFS retained less footprint-mean heat deficit at every hour under all seven definitions, with mean paired effects of approximately $-1.2$\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$ under both feedback modes (Table~\ref{tab:v3-heatdeficit}).
Every run ended with more deficit at 1800 than at 1400 UTC, despite within-window declines (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-heatdeficit-traj}).
The weaker-state criterion therefore passed and the faster-loss criterion failed.

\subsection{Process Treatments on the Fine-Terrain Branch}
\label{sec:v3-process}

Replacing YSU/revised-MM5 with Mellor--Yamada--Janji\'c (MYJ)/Eta schemes \citep{Janjic1994} on fine terrain with slope radiation (X2 minus X1) increased temperature bias by 0.37\,$^{\circ}$C, reduced wind absolute error by 0.145\,m\,s$^{-1}$ and increased layer-mean wind-vector magnitude by 0.098\,m\,s$^{-1}$.
All changes retained their sign across the five hours but were smaller than the forcing-product effects on shared lower-layer metrics.
The opposing temperature- and wind-error changes preclude an overall scheme ranking.

Adding slope radiation and shading (X1 minus X0) had negligible effects at basin-floor station columns but produced a spatially organised sidewall skin-temperature response.
The archived flat-plane shortwave field and missing surface heat fluxes prevented a direct mechanism test or configuration recommendation.

\subsection{Nest-Feedback Robustness across Two Terrain Regimes}
\label{sec:v3-feedback}

\label{sec:v3-cmp06}
Default-terrain feedback effects were small for both products: one-way minus two-way contrasts were at most 0.07\,K in endpoint difference and 0.10\,m\,s$^{-1}$ in wind-vector magnitude (Table~\ref{tab:v3-effects}).
The fine-terrain NAM comparison (X9 minus X0, two-way minus one-way) was similarly small, with block-sensitivity bounds spanning zero for both metrics.
Surface wind and pressure changes retained their signs but were also small, providing no feedback recommendation beyond this case.

\subsection{Terrain-Source Brackets}
\label{sec:v3-terrain}

The terrain comparisons hold NAM forcing, one-way feedback, WRF grid spacing and YSU/MM5 physics fixed.
Default minus fine (X3 minus X0) had a small d02 response and failed its predeclared terrain-response test.
The wider default-minus-coarse (X3 minus X4) and fine-minus-coarse (X0 minus X4) brackets gave larger spatial responses.
For fine minus coarse, the heat-deficit median changed sign between d02 (+0.014\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$) and d03 ($-0.184$\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$), while the two d03 section medians were +0.278 and +0.369\,K.
Figure~\ref{fig:v3-terrain-cmp11} shows this domain-dependent response without establishing terrain-source superiority, drainage pathways or basin cold-air-volume change.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:v3-discussion}

\subsection{Forcing-Product Differences in the Lower Layer}
\label{sec:v3-disc-supplied-state}

The principal result is the repeated GFS--NAM separation under both feedback modes: GFS has a smaller potential-temperature endpoint difference, larger layer-mean wind-vector magnitude and less heat deficit.
These diagnostics are consistent with weaker confinement, while pressure and wind errors prevent an overall skill ranking.
Because confinement, emissions, snow, cloud, photolysis and deposition errors can compensate in coupled simulations \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm,Neemann2015-ws,Matichuk2017-bq}, forcing-product sensitivity deserves attention in a future chemistry ensemble.
Its importance across other episodes remains to be tested.

The modelled d03 Horsepool profiles show product-specific initial states and first-hour evolution (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-spinup-profiles}).
They combine early model adjustment with lateral-boundary evolution and lack colocated observed validation.

\subsection{Heat-Deficit Evolution and Spatial Extent}
\label{sec:v3-disc-spatial}

Lower GFS heat deficit persisted across all definitions, but positive net changes in every run rule out demonstrated faster loss during the retained window.
At least a full diurnal cycle is needed to test differences in cold-pool erosion or persistence.
The forcing-product response was strongest below 500\,m AGL and consistent in its footprint mean, while the failed every-hour spatial-coherence test limits its spatial generality.

\subsection{Physics, Feedback and Terrain Sensitivities}
\label{sec:v3-disc-process}

Physics and feedback effects were smaller than the forcing-product response on the common temperature and wind diagnostics.
The scheme treatment improved wind error while increasing temperature bias; feedback remained small on both terrain branches.
Slope radiation and terrain changes produced spatial responses that station metrics could miss.
These results favour combining station and spatial diagnostics, while leaving radiation and terrain-source recommendations unresolved.

\subsection{Observations and Future Experiments}
\label{sec:v3-disc-domains}

Independent vertical profiles are needed to evaluate inversion depth, layer stability and heat deficit; neither surface contrasts nor scheme-dependent native PBL height provide that validation.
A bounded search of accessible holdings found no measured in-basin profile within the retained window.
The University of Utah's provisional Roosevelt sounding began at 1801:54 UTC, just after the window, and was neither quality controlled nor compared with WRF here \citep{UtahUBOS}.
Using it requires an explicit time tolerance, quality control and matched analysis.
Further work should separate initial from lateral-boundary forcing, test common-support d02--d03 responses, compare nudging with independent verification, and evaluate multiple cold-pool episodes.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:v3-conclusions}

The GFS--NAM contrast produced the largest repeated lower-layer response on the common d02 sample.
Under both feedback modes, GFS was warmer at the evaluated surface sites, had an approximately 3.6\,K smaller 50--500\,m potential-temperature endpoint difference, a 0.8--1.0\,m\,s$^{-1}$ larger wind-vector magnitude and approximately 1.2\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$ less footprint-mean heat deficit.
Scalar speed retained the wind ordering, while varying directional alignment supports no transport interpretation.
The heat-deficit separation represents a state offset without demonstrated faster loss; surface errors do not yield an overall product ranking.

Feedback effects were small (at most 0.07\,K and 0.10\,m\,s$^{-1}$ on the same layer metrics), while MYJ/Eta increased temperature bias by 0.37\,$^{\circ}$C and reduced wind absolute error by 0.145\,m\,s$^{-1}$.
Slope radiation and terrain effects depended on spatial support: the fine-minus-coarse heat-deficit median changed from +0.014\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$ on d02 to $-0.184$\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$ on d03.
These findings support complementary station, layer and footprint diagnostics for meteorology supplied to chemistry models.
Longer matched integrations and independent vertical observations are needed to identify forcing contributions and evaluate inversion depth and persistence; this study establishes neither basin export nor ozone response.
