\abstract{Cold-pool structure controls confinement of lower-atmospheric flow relevant to wintertime ozone and its precusors in Utah's Uinta Basin.
Previous studies have documented difficulty of capturing the stable cold pools in meteorological models, precluding an accurate air-quality simulation when pollutants blow away with a poorly represented cold pool.
Herein, we evaluate the meteorology behind one such high-ozone event on 2 February 2013 with Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) simulations. 
We test sensitivity to Global Forecast System (GFS) or North American Mesoscale (NAM) forcing; further experiments changed domain feedback, boundary-layer and surface-layer schemes, slope-aware radiation, and terrain datasets (fine versus coarse).
Across five hours, GFS-driven runs were warmer at the evaluated surface sites, had an approximately 3.6\,K smaller 50--500\,m potential-temperature difference, and $\approx1.0\,m\,s^{-1}$ larger layer-mean wind-vector magnitude than NAM-driven runs.
GFS also retained less mean heat-deficit (i.e., a weaker cold-pool in which to trap pollution precursors).
The cold pool strengthened in all runs over the six hours, hence the product separation suggests a state difference rather than necessarily faster erosion of the cold-pool.
Conversely, toggling feedback between domains and parameterisation settings yielder smaller responses; terrain fineness predominately increased surface roughness and hence theoretically slower drainage of cold air. 
The results guide simulation configurations for subsequent air-chemistry modelling, while leaving the effects of multiple forcing contributions, and uncertainty over cold-air depth and export, for future studies.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:v3-introduction}

Wintertime ozone in the Uinta Basin develops within snow-covered, stable lower-atmospheric layers that confine precursors near the surface \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm,Neemann2015-ws,Davies2025-wy}.
The basin floor, benches and surrounding terrain support strong temperature contrasts and thermally driven slope flows \citep{Lyman2015-aq,Whiteman2000}.
Comparable winter ozone episodes are rare in the United States, but have been documented in, e.g., Wyoming's oil-and-gas-producing basins \citep{Schnell2009-pe,Oltmans2014-md} and in northern China \citep{Li2021-xk}.
Herein, we examine the meteorology of a well documented 2013 high-ozone episode \citep{Mansfield2020-kl}, often poorly simulated in these adjacent studies \citep{Neemann2015-ws,Lyman2015-aq,Matichuk2017-bq,Tran2018-cx}, over five hours after sunrise during which a shallow nocturnal cold pool must battle convective overturning to persist and continue capture of ozone and its precursors. 

Surface observations alone cannot estimate cold-pool depth or the three-dimensional mixing volume supplied to a chemistry model.
Evaluation therefore requires complementary diagnostics of potential temperature, lower-layer winds, turbulent fluxes, and (positive, negative) heat storage, with a clear distinction between observed agreement and modelled structure.

Earlier Uinta Basin ozone modelling demonstrates strong case dependence; hence, general conclusions cannot be drawn individually, but we highlight some best practices relating to air-quality-critical meteorology as a foundation alongside further improvement in air-chemistry model configuration \citep{Stajner2022-uw,Huang2025-kv,Ivey2019-wn}.
Common to many cases in the Basin \citep{Neemann2015-ws,Mansfield2024-lc} is the result that snow cover can strengthen cold pools by increasing albedo and hence critical to simulate---a notoriously difficult task for previous numerical simulations---while atmospheric water phase modulates longwave forcing and nocturnal cooling \citep{Neemann2015-ws,Hallar2021-yt}.
Further compounding potential error, choice of prescribed microphysics and domain configuration alters both the cold pool and its representation on atmospheric-chemistry grids.
Four-dimensional data assimilation (FDDA) depends on the nudged variables, input analysis and scheme-dependent boundary-layer diagnosis \citep{Tran2018FDDA}.
A subsequent study examined reinitialisation, snow initialisation, and nudging, but assimilated evaluation data and differing samples limit configuration comparisons \citep{Tran2021-al}.
These studies, with results from unpublished laboratory reports, motivate the present sensitivity tests: \textit{can we improve the meteorology that is necessary to simulate a persistent cold-pool and high-ozone event}?

Errors in meteorology, emission inventory, photolysis, etc. may cancel in coupled simulations.
While these errors are difficult to extricate, a commonality is poor representation of persistent cold-air pools \citep{Tran2018-cx,Neemann2015-ws}.
Changing emissions with fixed meteorology can improve ozone agreement without uniquely identifying inventory error \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm}.
Cumulative changes to these processes complicate attribution, and improved temperature profiles need not improve ozone profiles \citep{Matichuk2017-bq}.
Hence credible meteorology reduces this confounding but does not guarantee improved ozone forecasts.

Deploying the Weather Research and Forecast (WRF) model \citep{Skamarock2019-dk}, we test whether the lower-atmospheric response to two different sets of weather models' initial and lateral-boundary conditions recurs under one-way (i.e., top-down) and two-way nest feedback, and compare it with parameterisation-suite and terrain-fineness responses.
The diagnostics combine surface skill, the 50--500\,m potential-temperature difference, layer-mean horizontal wind-vector magnitude, and mean heat deficit over a delineated area.
Together, they describe the simulated cold-pool state, but in focussing our scope we do not estimate ventilation or basin export herein.

We ask:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Does the lower-layer response to different weather-model forcing datasets recur under both feedback modes?
  \item How do changes in domain feedback, boundary-/surface-layer schemes, and slope-aware parameterisations compare with the response to different weather-model forcings?
  \item How does changing the terrain dataset fineness affect the same scenario?
  \item What do the combined diagnostics reveal that in-situ stations may not?
\end{enumerate}

The study contains no chemistry calculation and does not separate initial from lateral-boundary forcing; follow-on work in preparation will connect meteorological representation with air-chemistry modelling for this and related cases.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:v3-methods}

\subsection{Case, Observations, and Evaluation Domains}
\label{sec:v3-case}

The case falls within the January--February 2013 period of snow cover, cold pools and elevated Uinta ozone \citep{Neemann2015-ws,Ahmadov2015-zm,Tran2018FDDA}.
Integrations capture 1200--1800 Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), or 0500--1100 Mountain Standard Time (MST; UTC$-7$) on 2 February 2013.
Excluding the initialised output and first-hour forecast to allow spin-up leaves the window 1400--1800 UTC (0700--1100 MST) inclusive (i.e., five hourly samples).
This was chosen as a typical time for cold-pool breakup if sufficient convective overturning occurs.
Data from in-situ stations acquired from Synoptic Data include 2\,m temperature, 10\,m wind speed, and surface pressure \citep{SynopticTimeseries}.
Subhourly reports were averaged within each UTC clock hour.
The stations span the basin floor, sidewalls and benches but are unevenly distributed and are for the reader's reference rather than rigorous skill assessment.
%We use the 1\,km d02 domain for because seven stations lie outside the usable d03 interior (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-locator}); a common-support d02--d03 comparison remains future work.
Four stations provide context from 31 January 0000 UTC to 4 February 0000 UTC: for the observed and modelled difference between crest and basin (Fig.~\ref{fig:v3-episode-context}), we calculate surface potential temperature as $\theta=T(1000/p)^{0.2854}$, with $T$ in kelvin and $p$ in hPa, using the dry-air Poisson relation \citep{WallaceHobbs2006}.

Ozone context uses the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Air Quality System (AQS) AirData daily archive for January--February 2013 \citep{EPAAirData}.
We retained the ``Ozone 8-hour 2015'' daily-summary row at Ouray, Redwash, Roosevelt, Vernal and Little Mountain: one row per site on each of 59 local dates, with within-day coverage of 47--100\% marked in the figure.
The satellite panel below (Fig.~\ref{fig:v3-episode-context}) uses the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Global Imagery Browse Services (GIBS) Terra Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) corrected-reflectance 7-2-1 layer for the period of interest \citep{NASAGIBS}.
We use spectral absorption and scattering principles described by \citet{Liou2002}, with snow/cloud colours specified by NASA's layer documentation \citep{NASAMODIS721} to inform interpretation.

\subsection{WRF Configuration and Archived Simulation Family}
\label{sec:v3-family}

We used WRF version 4.8.0 \citep{Skamarock2019}, initialised at 1200 UTC with lateral boundary conditions through 1800 UTC on 2 February 2013.
The inputs were the 1200 and 1800 UTC zero-hour GFS grid 004 (0.5$^{\circ}$; \texttt{gfsanl\_4}) or NAM grid 218 (12\,km; \texttt{namanl\_218}) analyses \citep{GFSAnalysis,NAMAnalysis}.
These integrations include temporally interpolated lateral-boundary forcing, so the retained response combines initial-state and boundary influences.
Three nested domains, d01--d03, have horizontal spacings of 3\,km, 1\,km and 333.3\,m, with 75 model (eta) levels. 
Note that GFS and NAM inputs supply 27 and 40 metgrid levels, respectively.
This yielded approximately 20\,m vertical spacing near the surface; we prescribed a 50-hPa model top.
The control physics-parameterisation suite comprises Thompson microphysics \citep{Thompson2008-ti}, Rapid Radiative Transfer Model for General Circulation Models (RRTMG) longwave and shortwave radiation \citep{Iacono2008}, the revised fifth-generation Pennsylvania State University--National Center for Atmospheric Research Mesoscale Model (MM5) similarity surface layer \citep{Jimenez2012Surface}, the Noah land-surface model \citep{Tewari2004} and the Yonsei University (YSU) planetary boundary layer (PBL) scheme \citep{Hong2006}. 
No nudging or data assimilation was used.
Table~\ref{tab:v3-family} lists the simulations analysed here.

\subsection{Surface and Common-AGL Diagnostics}
\label{sec:v3-common-agl}

Surface variables and vertical fields were sampled from the same nearest d02 ($\Delta x = 1~\text{km}$ horizontal spacing) column at each station, keeping the model terrain and profile consistent.
Station-to-cell distances are 133--594\,m, with default-terrain elevation offsets of $-32.4$ to $+14.3$\,m (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v3-stations}).
These pairings retain differences in exposure, slope and land cover; alternative interpolation methods were not tested.
% Mass-level height above ground level (AGL) was calculated from perturbation plus base geopotential relative to local terrain.
% Winds were destaggered and rotated to earth-relative components.
Potential temperature was interpolated at the layer bounds; wind components were linearly interpolated every 25\,m from 50 to 500\,m above ground level (AGL) for height integration.
We repeat the eleven-column, five-hour comparison for 50--250 and 100--500\,m layers using 10-m and 50-m vertical-spacing integration increments.
The bounds define a common shallow layer rather than an observed inversion boundary.

The finite-layer potential-temperature difference is defined as

\begin{equation}
S_{50:500}=\theta(500\,\mathrm{m})-\theta(50\,\mathrm{m})
\end{equation}

An increase of potential temperature with height indicates dry static stability \citep{WallaceHobbs2006,Stull1988}.
In addition, the magnitude of the layer-mean horizontal wind vector was defined as

\begin{equation}
U_{50:500}=\left(\bar{u}^{2}+\bar{v}^{2}\right)^{1/2},
\end{equation}

where the bars denote height means from 50\,m to 500\,m AGL, not temporal or turbulent averages.
We also calculate layer-mean scalar speed $\overline{(u^{2}+v^{2})^{1/2}}$ and vector-to-scalar ratio to estimate compounding errors in direction.
Native height of the planetary boundary layer (PBL), as simulated by WRF, serves as a scheme-dependent companion diagnostic \citep{Tran2018FDDA} but displayed a number of spurious artefacts (not shown) that precluded useful assessment herein.

\subsection{Pairing, Aggregation, and Short-Sample Sensitivity}
\label{sec:v3-aggregation}

Run differences were paired by location and valid hour, then reduced to one spatial median per hour.
Reported five-hour effects are means of those medians; stations are not independent spatially.
To explore temporal dependence \citep{Wilks2019} and confidence thereof, a circular moving-block calculation drew 20,000 resamples of the five medians using two-hour blocks.
Its 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles are descriptive sensitivity bounds given the singular case; the authors submit it is practical rather than statistical significance that  represents more value in application.

\subsection{Area-Integrated Heat Deficit}
\label{sec:v3-heatdeficit-methods}

To represent the cold-pool strength numerically, we adapted the basin heat-deficit formulation of \citet{Whiteman1999} and the column implementation of \citet{Adler2023}.
Using hydrostatic balance, $dp=-\rho g\,dz$, to express column mass in pressure coordinates \citep{WallaceHobbs2006}, we defined
\begin{equation}
H=\frac{c_{p}}{g}\int_{p_{c}}^{p_{s}}\max\{\theta_{\mathrm{crest}}-\theta(p),0\}\,dp,
\end{equation}
where $c_{p}$ is dry-air specific heat at constant pressure, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and $p_s>p_c$ are surface and crest-reference pressures.
The reference potential temperature is interpolated to the fixed crest height in each modelled column; clipping retains only positive deficits.
This potential-temperature proxy then describes stratification relative to that reference.
We report its cell-area-weighted mean in MJ\,m$^{-2}$: this primary area is the convex hull of eleven stations and eleven basin waypoints (Fig.~\ref{fig:v3-locator}), restricted to terrain below 2200\,m \footnote{4,965 d02 cells covering 5,277\,km$^{2}$.}.
We tested sensitivity of results to this hull at 2100, 2200 and 2300\,m; increasing or eroding the area by one cell; and station-only areas at 2200\,m.
Given sensitivity of the case to complex terrain, we ensure GFS--NAM contrasts share a terrain dataset.

\label{sec:v3-results}

\subsection{Observed Episode Context}
\label{sec:v3-episode-context}

The MODIS image shows extensive snow and ice across the basin and surrounding terrain (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-episode-context}a).
The AQS series places 2 February within a broader period of elevated ozone, with daily maximum eight-hour concentrations of 56--95\,ppb across the five sites (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-episode-context}b).
These observations establish episode context without attributing ozone to the simulated meteorology.


\subsection{Observed Surface-Temperature Context}
\label{sec:v3-observed-context}

Inspecting a sample of observation stations across a range of altitudes, a representative basin-floor station repeatedly separated from the higher sites between 31 January and 3 February, with strong variation in diurnal temperature differences (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-observed}).
Seep Ridge (USU04; 1933\,m) appears anomalous, despite a modest station-to-cell distance of 133\,m and 3\,m terrain offset in the vertical, showing latent limitations in estimating cold-pool strength with observations.
Exposure, slope flow and representativeness are possible explanations, but the metadata do not distinguish them, and is outside the present scope.
The recurring floor-to-bench separation is consistent with a cold-pool surface pattern; however, the horizontally separated sites cannot determine its vertical structure as evidenced in the following.

\subsection{Driving-Product Response in the Lower Layer}
\label{sec:v3-driving-response}

Runs forced with GFS produced a smaller 50--500\,m potential-temperature difference than NAM under both feedback modes (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-retention}).
Paired GFS-minus-NAM effects averaged $-3.63$\,K with two-way feedback and $-3.65$\,K with one-way feedback, consistency borne out by a constant sign at every hour (Table~\ref{tab:v3-effects}).
Layer-mean wind-vector magnitude increased by 0.79 and 0.99\,m\,s$^{-1}$, respectively, also with consistent hourly signs.
Scalar-speed differences were similar, and directional cancellation therefore likely does not create the speed ordering (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v3-wind-cancellation}).
The vector-to-scalar ratio difference was not consistent and hence not analysed further.

Temperature and wind trend in the same directions across all experiments shown herein.
Their magnitudes depend on layer bounds (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v3-layer-sensitivity}); sensitivity tests that varied integration increment at fixed bounds altered mean wind by $< 0.02 m s^{-1}$.
Native PBL-height differences change sign under both feedback modes, suggesting a weak signal, also consistent with the diagnostic's scheme dependence discussed in \citep{Tran2018FDDA}.

\subsection{Fine-Terrain Experiments}
\label{sec:v3-process}

Replacing YSU/revised-MM5 with Mellor--Yamada--Janji\'c (MYJ)/Eta schemes \citep{Janjic1994} on fine terrain with slope radiation (X2 minus X1) increased temperature bias by 0.37\,$^{\circ}$C, reduced wind absolute error by 0.145\,m\,s$^{-1}$ and increased layer-mean wind-vector magnitude by 0.098\,m\,s$^{-1}$.
All changes were consistent in the 5-h period, but were smaller than the forcing-product impact.
The inconsistent temperature- and wind-error changes preclude further commentary on overall scheme behaviour.

Adding slope radiation and shading (X1 minus X0) had negligible effects at basin-floor station columns but produced a spatially organised sidewall skin-temperature response.
The increased roughness of high-resolution terrain slowed cold-air drainage in preliminary experiments across numerous grid-spacing configurations (not shown).
This is related to the ill-defined length or surface area of a fractal \citep{Mandelbrot1967-gq}, where increased spatial resolution does not materially increase volume to hold cold air, yet \textit{does} diverge as the grid fineness tends to zero. 
A finer terrain and three-dimensional resolution in numerical integration should theoretically resolve finer reservoirs and sinks for cold air that delay onset of drainage flow.
Testing the sensitivity of cold-air drainage speed to surface roughness and terrain fineness is outside the present scope.

\subsection{Nest-Feedback and Terrain Fineness}
\label{sec:v3-feedback}

\label{sec:v3-cmp06}
Effects introduced by toggling domain two-way feedback were small for both products: one-way minus two-way contrasts were at most 0.07\,K in bulk potential-temperature difference and 0.10\,m\,s$^{-1}$ in wind-vector magnitude (Table~\ref{tab:v3-effects}).
The fine-terrain NAM comparison (X9 minus X0, two-way minus one-way) was similarly small, with block-sensitivity bounds spanning zero for both metrics.
Surface wind and pressure changes were negligible, providing no feedback recommendation beyond this case.

\subsection{Terrain-Source Tests}
\label{sec:v3-terrain}

The terrain comparisons hold NAM forcing, one-way feedback, WRF grid spacing and YSU/MM5 physics fixed as control.
Default-minus-fine (X3 minus X0) had a small d02 response, while a wider default-minus-coarse (X3 minus X4) and fine-minus-coarse (X0 minus X4) terrain experiment gave larger spatial responses.
For fine minus coarse, the heat-deficit median changed sign (as a proxy for consistency) between d02 (+0.014\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$) and d03 ($-0.184$\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$), while the two d03 section medians were +0.278 and +0.369\,K.
Figure~\ref{fig:v3-terrain-cmp11} shows this domain-dependent response; further animations in time confirmed a slower drainage of cold air as terrain resolution was increased (not shown). The correspondence of $\Delta x$ and drainage speed, its generality beyond one case, and whether this consistently leads to delayed cold-pool development is a matter of future study.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:v3-discussion}

\subsection{Forcing-Product Differences in the Lower Layer}
\label{sec:v3-disc-supplied-state}

The principal result is the GFS--NAM separation in numerous runs: GFS has a smaller potential-temperature layer difference, larger layer-mean wind-vector magnitude, and less heat deficit (weaker cold pool).
These diagnostics are consistent with weaker confinement, and likely poorer containment of ozone precursors given the cold air's reticence of persistence.
Because emissions, snow, cloud, and photolysis/chemical-mechanism errors can compensate in coupled simulations \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm,Neemann2015-ws,Matichuk2017-bq}, forcing-product sensitivity deserves focus in future air-chemistry simulations; the authors thus recommend a Monte-Carlo (ensemble) simulation to capture inter-case variation in meteorological forcing.

The modelled d03 Horsepool profiles, sampled near the lowest point in the Basin (and cold-air sink), show product-specific initial states and first-hour evolution (Figure~\ref{fig:v3-spinup-profiles}).
The large change in the spin-up time overwhelms any error seen elsewhere in the authors' experiments; neglecting a robust assessment of the meteorology dataset before adding chemistry places analysis on shaky ground due to the large variation between cases in internal laboratory reports (not shown).

\subsection{Heat-Deficit Evolution and Spatial Extent}
\label{sec:v3-disc-spatial}

Lower GFS-run heat deficit persisted across all sensitivity tests, but positive net changes in every run make this less likely related to a faster heat loss during the window of interest.
Indeed, at least a full diurnal cycle is needed to test differences in cold-pool erosion or persistence, and is a topic of the authors' current research.
The forcing-product response was strongest below 500\,m AGL and consistent in its mean effect, while lack of consistency in space (specifics not shown) limits its spatial generality.

\subsection{Parameterisation, Feedback, and Terrain Sensitivities}
\label{sec:v3-disc-process}

Parameterisation and feedback effects were smaller than the forcing-product response on the temperature and wind diagnostics.
The changes in parameterisation configuration modulated wind error while increasing temperature separation; feedback remained small on both terrain branches.
While changes were small, slope radiation and terrain-fineness effects produced spatial responses that in-situ stations could miss.
These results favour combining station and spatial diagnostics for a fully three-dimensional analysis (and advancing in time).

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:v3-conclusions}

We have presented output of WRF runs to highlight the sensitivity of cold-pool representation---critical for the retention of ozone and its precursors---to initial weather-model forcing.
We use a well documented Winter 2013 case in the Uinta Basin as a foundation for finding evidence of large differences in meteorology simulations.
The GFS--NAM contrast produced the largest repeated lower-layer response on the d02 (1-km) domain.
Under both feedback modes, GFS-driven runs were warmer at the evaluated surface sites, had an approximately 3.6\,K smaller 50--500\,m potential-temperature difference, a 0.8--1.0\,m\,s$^{-1}$ larger wind-vector magnitude and approximately 1.2\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$ less area-mean heat deficit (weaker cold pool).
It follows the heat-deficit separation likely represents a state offset rather than demonstrated faster heat loss.

Nest-feedback effects were small (at most 0.07\,K and 0.10\,m\,s$^{-1}$ on the same layer metrics), while MYJ/Eta increased temperature bias by 0.37\,$^{\circ}$C and reduced wind absolute error by 0.145\,m\,s$^{-1}$.
Slope radiation and terrain effects depended on the spatial definition: the fine-minus-coarse heat-deficit median changed from +0.014\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$ on d02 to $-0.184$\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$ on d03.
These findings support complementary station, layer and Basin-wide diagnostics for meteorology supplied to chemistry models; special focus should be paid to the uncertainty dimension, also, through running Monte Carlo (ensemble) experiments.
Longer matched integrations and independent vertical observations are needed to identify forcing contributions and evaluate inversion depth and persistence; this study establishes neither basin export nor ozone response, for instance.
