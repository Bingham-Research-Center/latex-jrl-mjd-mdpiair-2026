\Title{WRF Cold-Pool Meteorology during a Uinta Basin Winter Ozone Episode: Sensitivity to Driving Data and Nest Feedback}

\abstract{Cold-air-pool structure determines the mixing environment supplied to a winter-ozone chemistry model, yet surface skill alone does not establish whether a simulation retains a shallow, weakly ventilated layer.
We evaluated four deterministic Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) simulations of the 2 February 2013 Uinta Basin episode in a two-by-two design that crossed GFS and NAM driving products with one-way and two-way nest feedback.
The common evaluation used eleven fixed station columns on the 1 km domain and five post-spin-up hours from 14:00 to 18:00 UTC.
Relative to NAM, GFS increased the paired 2 m-temperature-bias contrast by 1.79 and 1.76~$^{\circ}$C under two-way and one-way feedback, respectively.
The corresponding GFS-minus-NAM differences in 50--500 m above-ground-level potential-temperature stability were -3.63 and -3.65 K, while fixed-layer transport-wind differences were +0.79 and +0.99 m s$^{-1}$.
The stability and transport-wind contrasts retained the same sign in all five hours under both feedback modes.
Within-product feedback effects and their interaction were substantially smaller for these layer diagnostics.
For this short sample, the driving-product contrast therefore exceeded the nest-feedback response in the meteorological state relevant to dilution and retention.
The experiment does not separate initial-state from lateral-boundary effects, establish multi-day cold-pool persistence, validate inversion depth against an observed profile, or quantify an ozone response.}

\section{Introduction}

Elevated winter ozone in Utah's Uinta Basin develops when photochemical production occurs within a persistent, snow-covered cold-air pool.
Snow increases actinic flux and modifies deposition, while stable, weakly ventilated air confines ozone and its precursors near the basin floor \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm,Neemann2015-ws,Davies2025-wy}.
A chemistry simulation can therefore reproduce neither the relevant dilution volume nor the residence time of emitted precursors if its meteorological driver erodes the cold pool too quickly.

The chemical and meteorological uncertainties cannot be ranked independently from the existing literature.
Ahmadov et al. found that a top-down oil-and-gas emissions scenario greatly improved simulated ozone, but explicitly conditioned part of the inventory interpretation on adequate meteorology \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm}.
Matichuk et al. likewise found strong emissions and deposition sensitivities, while noting that improved WRF temperature profiles rarely produced improved ozone profiles \citep{Matichuk2017-bq}.
These studies show that emissions, chemistry, deposition, photolysis, and transport can compensate for one another.
They support meteorological evaluation as a prerequisite for chemical attribution, not a claim that meteorology is the unique source of winter-ozone error.

Meteorology-focused work presents a similarly conditional picture.
For the same 2013 episode, Neemann et al. showed case-specific sensitivity to snow and cloud-ice treatments, although the cloud modification was deliberately a bounding experiment and the meteorological grid used by the chemistry model was coarser than the principal evaluation grid \citep{Neemann2015-ws}.
Tran et al. showed that observational and analysis nudging could improve some aspects of the simulated inversion while degrading others, depending on the nudged variables, data source, and diagnosed boundary-layer depth \citep{Tran2018-ax}.
Consequently, labels such as ``NAM driven,'' ``nudged,'' or ``high resolution'' are not sufficient descriptions of a controlled experiment.

The present study addresses a narrower question: how strongly does the short-window meteorological state respond to the driving-data product, and does that response persist under two nest-feedback settings?
We compare GFS- and NAM-driven WRF simulations in a reconciled two-by-two design and evaluate surface error together with common-height lower-tropospheric stability and wind.
The analysis tests the meteorological structure that would be supplied to a later chemistry model.
It does not test chemical production, determine which driving product is generally superior, or establish cold-pool persistence beyond the five verified post-spin-up hours.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Case, Observations, and Evaluation Sample}

The case is the snow-covered Uinta Basin cold-air pool of 2 February 2013, within the multi-day episode studied by Neemann et al. and Ahmadov et al. \citep{Neemann2015-ws,Ahmadov2015-zm}.
All model comparisons use 14:00--18:00 UTC, or 07:00--11:00 mountain standard time.
The first two forecast hours were excluded as spin-up, leaving five common hourly samples.
The sensitivity of the result to that two-hour choice has not yet been evaluated.

Surface observations were extracted from the Synoptic archive for eleven stations reporting 2 m temperature, ten reporting 10 m wind speed, and nine reporting surface pressure during every retained hour.
Subhourly reports were averaged within each UTC clock hour.
The fixed sample includes basin-floor, sidewall, and bench locations, but it is neither spatially uniform nor area weighted.
The 1 km second domain (d02) was used because the 333 m third domain excludes most higher bench stations.
Observed temperature from 31 January 00:00 UTC through 4 February 00:00 UTC provides multi-day context around the modeled window.

Observed and modeled surface potential temperature was calculated as $\theta=T(1000/p)^{0.2854}$, with $T$ in kelvin and $p$ in hPa.
The elevation-spanning contrast was defined as Mountain Home on the northwest bench minus Pariette Draw on the basin floor.
Because the stations are horizontally separated and locally exposed surface sites, this difference is a structural index rather than a colocated free-air inversion strength or depth.

\subsection{WRF Experiment Matrix}

The four simulations used WRF version 4.8.0 and ran from 12:00 to 18:00 UTC on 2 February 2013.
Three nested domains had horizontal grid spacing of 3, 1, and 0.333 km, 75 eta levels, and a 50 hPa model top.
The physics suite comprised Thompson microphysics, RRTMG longwave and shortwave radiation, the revised MM5 surface layer, the Noah land-surface model, and the YSU planetary-boundary-layer scheme.
All simulations were free forecasts with grid and observation FDDA disabled.
They used the same domain geometry, default 30-arc-second-class terrain field, physics, initialization time, output cadence, and valid window.

The factorial crossed the archived GFS analysis product \texttt{gfsanl\_4} with the NAM analysis product \texttt{namanl\_218}, and crossed one-way (\texttt{feedback=0}) with two-way (\texttt{feedback=1}) nest feedback (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}).
The products supplied zero-hour analyses at 12:00 and 18:00 UTC and had 27 and 40 metgrid levels for GFS and NAM, respectively.
Thus, the driving-data contrast is a product-level treatment that includes the analyses' native horizontal and vertical information; it is not an isolated perturbation of one atmospheric variable.
Within each product family, normalized as-run namelists differed only in the feedback flag.
The GFS one-way run reused the six verified metgrid files from the GFS two-way run before rerunning \texttt{real.exe} and \texttt{wrf.exe}.

\subsection{Surface, Retention, and Ventilation Diagnostics}

Surface temperature, earth-relative 10 m wind, and surface pressure were sampled at the nearest d02 cell to each station.
Across the 11 cells, model terrain minus station elevation had a median of -2.0 m and a range of -32.4 to +14.3 m.
This close elevation match reduces one source of representativeness error but does not account for local exposure or unresolved terrain.

Mass-level height above ground was calculated from perturbation and base-state geopotential and the local WRF terrain height.
Grid-relative winds were destaggered and rotated to earth-relative components.
Potential temperature and both wind components were then linearly interpolated every 25 m from 50 to 500 m above ground level (AGL) at each station column.
Fixed-layer stability was defined as $S_{50:500}=\theta(500\,\mathrm{m})-\theta(50\,\mathrm{m})$.
The transport-wind proxy was $U_{50:500}=\{\bar{u}^{2}+\bar{v}^{2}\}^{1/2}$, where the bars denote height means from 50 to 500 m AGL.
This proxy represents the mean wind available for horizontal transport within a fixed lower layer.
It is not a calculation of flow across the basin boundary, pollutant residence time, cold-pool volume, or a chemistry-model ventilation coefficient.
Native WRF PBL height was retained as a scheme-dependent companion diagnostic rather than interpreted as an observed layer depth.

\subsection{Aggregation and Short-Sample Interpretation}

Run differences were paired by station and valid hour before spatial aggregation.
The forcing-by-feedback interaction was defined as \([\mathrm{GFS}_{1\mathrm{w}}-\mathrm{GFS}_{2\mathrm{w}}]-[\mathrm{NAM}_{1\mathrm{w}}-\mathrm{NAM}_{2\mathrm{w}}]\) and was likewise evaluated at each station and hour before spatial aggregation.
The fixed station field was reduced to one unweighted spatial median per hour, and the five hourly medians were then averaged for each effect summary.
The stations were therefore kept together as one spatial field rather than treated as independent replicates.
Hourly ranges and sign counts accompany the means because the five values are visible in the time-series figure.

A two-hour circular moving-block resample of the five hourly medians was also repeated 20,000 times as a descriptive sensitivity calculation.
Those ranges are retained in the reproducibility record but are not confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, or estimates of observation, forcing, or structural model uncertainty.
No formal population inference is attempted from four deterministic integrations of one case.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Observed Cold-Pool Context}

Pariette Draw on the basin floor repeatedly cooled below the higher sites, while the magnitude and ordering of the station temperatures changed through the diurnal cycle (Figure~\ref{fig:observed-evolution}).
This recurring spatial separation is consistent with cold-pool conditions during the modeled episode.
It does not by itself establish the depth, volume, or vertical temperature structure of the cold pool.

\subsection{Response to the Driving-Data Product}

Under two-way feedback, the GFS-driven run had a smaller Mountain Home-minus-Pariette Draw potential-temperature contrast than the matched NAM-driven run in every retained hour.
The observed spatial contrast ranged from 12.63 to 20.21 K, the NAM run from 16.44 to 18.07 K, and the GFS run from 7.35 to 11.74 K.
These two-site values are not interpreted as free-air inversion depths.

The surface evaluation showed the same product ordering under both feedback settings.
The mean hourly station-median 2 m-temperature biases were +2.09 and +2.51~$^{\circ}$C for the two GFS members, compared with +0.40 and +0.60~$^{\circ}$C for the corresponding NAM members.
After pairing by station and hour, GFS minus NAM increased the temperature-bias contrast by +1.79~$^{\circ}$C under two-way feedback and +1.76~$^{\circ}$C under one-way feedback (Table~\ref{tab:effects}).
The paired absolute-temperature-error differences were +1.41 and +1.50~$^{\circ}$C, respectively.
GFS minus NAM also increased 10 m-wind bias by +0.91 and +0.96 m s$^{-1}$, although the hourly difference in absolute wind error changed sign.

The common-AGL profiles separated the products more directly (Figure~\ref{fig:retention-ventilation}).
Mean GFS-minus-NAM 50--500 m stability was -3.63 K under two-way feedback and -3.65 K under one-way feedback.
The respective hourly spatial-median differences ranged from -5.58 to -1.13 K and from -5.36 to -0.90 K, retaining the same sign in all five hours.
Mean GFS-minus-NAM transport-wind differences were +0.79 and +0.99 m s$^{-1}$.
Their hourly values ranged from +0.37 to +1.18 m s$^{-1}$ and from +0.61 to +1.50 m s$^{-1}$, again with the same sign in all five hours.
In contrast, GFS-minus-NAM PBL-height differences changed sign within both feedback settings.

\subsection{Response to Nest Feedback}

The within-product feedback responses were much smaller than the GFS-minus-NAM contrasts, but they were not identical between products (Table~\ref{tab:effects}).
For NAM, one-way minus two-way averaged +0.002 K for stability and +0.006 m s$^{-1}$ for transport wind.
The NAM hourly effects changed sign and remained within -0.042 to +0.088 K and -0.035 to +0.033 m s$^{-1}$.

For GFS, one-way minus two-way averaged -0.066 K for stability and +0.104 m s$^{-1}$ for transport wind.
The stability effect ranged from -0.198 to +0.067 K and was negative in four of five hours, while the transport-wind effect ranged from +0.025 to +0.197 m s$^{-1}$ and was positive in all five hours.
The paired feedback interaction averaged -0.103 K for stability and +0.097 m s$^{-1}$ for transport wind.
The interaction stability changed sign once, while its transport-wind component remained positive in all five hours.
The 2 m-temperature-bias interaction was +0.041~$^{\circ}$C, and the PBL-height interaction also changed sign.

The evidence therefore bounds a small, product-conditioned feedback response on the present d02 sample.
It does not establish that one-way and two-way nesting are generally equivalent.

\section{Discussion}

Among the two factors tested, the driving-data product produced the larger response in surface temperature bias, fixed-layer stability, and transport wind.
The near-identical GFS-minus-NAM effects under one-way and two-way feedback make the product ordering robust to the feedback flag within this experiment.
They do not identify why the products differ.
The treatment bundles each analysis system's native grid, vertical information, and assimilated observations, and the WRF response combines the 12:00 UTC initial state with lateral-boundary evolution through 18:00 UTC.
A direct product-to-sounding comparison at initialization and a design that holds the boundary state fixed would be needed to separate those contributions.

The feedback result is narrower.
NAM one-way and two-way fields were nearly indistinguishable in the reported summaries, whereas the GFS transport-wind response and the paired interaction retained one sign but remained small compared with the driving-product contrast.
The common d02 station sample does not resolve whether larger feedback effects occur inside d03, near nest boundaries, later in the forecast, or under another synoptic regime.
The result should therefore be read as a bounded effect size, not evidence that nest feedback is unimportant in general.

For an air-chemistry application, the GFS members supplied a less stable and faster-moving 50--500 m layer during the verified window.
That combination is consistent with meteorological conditions more favorable to dilution than the NAM members, but the present wind metric does not demonstrate basin export or pollutant residence time.
This distinction matters because previous Uinta Basin studies show that meteorology, emissions, snow-enhanced photolysis, and deposition can compensate for one another \citep{Ahmadov2015-zm,Matichuk2017-bq}.
No chemistry was run here, so the meteorological contrast cannot be converted into an ozone effect or used to rank meteorology against inventory and mechanism errors.

The observational comparison also has an important scale mismatch.
The surface network supports a recurring spatial temperature contrast and evaluates fixed WRF columns, but it does not observe the modeled 50--500 m profile.
The Mountain Home-minus-Pariette Draw index combines elevation, horizontal distance, site exposure, and local surface energy balance.
An in-basin sounding, tethersonde, or validated remote-sensing profile would be required to evaluate inversion height and layer stability directly.
Documented provider quality control and station metadata are also required before the surface comparison is submission ready.

Temporal scope is the strongest limitation.
Five hours can show a reproducible separation among these deterministic integrations, but they cannot demonstrate multi-day persistence, cold-pool breakup, or conditions over the full ozone-accumulation period.
The two-hour spin-up exclusion has not been subjected to a sensitivity test, and the block resampling cannot create independent information that is absent from the short record.
The displayed hourly evolution and sign consistency are therefore more informative than whether a descriptive resampling range includes zero.

Other archived terrain, PBL, resolution, and nudging runs were excluded because they either require further analysis or do not isolate the advertised lever.
Future work should first extend the matched factorial through a provenance-checked multi-day period, add independent vertical observations, and distinguish initial from boundary-state effects.
Only then should the selected meteorological states be crossed with emissions and chemical mechanisms in a coupled experiment.

\section{Conclusions}

For the matched 2 February 2013 simulations, the driving-data product produced a larger response in the evaluated cold-pool metrics than the nest-feedback flag.
Relative to NAM, GFS was warmer at the surface, less stable from 50 to 500 m AGL, and faster in the fixed lower-layer transport-wind metric under both feedback settings.
The stability and wind differences retained the same sign in every evaluated hour, whereas within-product feedback effects were small on the common d02 station sample.
This result identifies a robust short-window separation among four specific simulations; it does not identify the responsible product property, validate inversion depth, establish event-scale persistence, calculate basin export, or predict ozone.
Longer matched integrations with independent vertical observations and controlled initial-versus-boundary-state tests are required before the meteorological result can support a coupled chemical attribution.
